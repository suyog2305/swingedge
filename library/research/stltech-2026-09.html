TenorFY27–FY29 (i.e. roughly April 2026 to March 2029) [1][2]
What is actually supplied“Optical connectivity products” for next-generation AI data centres [1] — STL’s own language across the press release and the call. Neither document specifies whether that means terminated cable assemblies, connectors and patch panels, bulk cable, or some mix; “connectivity” is used throughout rather than “fibre” or “cable” specifically, and this report cannot resolve the split any further.
Mechanism — the part that matters mostThis is an allocation, not a single firm purchase order. STL’s own disclosure describes the $1.11 billion as the contract’s value “based on prevailing selling prices,” with actual supply “allocated” across FY27–FY29 and purchase orders released periodically during the contract period [3][4]. The headline number is a ceiling computed at today’s prices, not a locked-in receivable.
Revenue recognition & marginNot disclosed with any specificity. Asked directly on the Q1 FY27 call, management confirmed data-centre solutions carry “higher margins than the telecom segment” but declined to quantify the gap: “we don’t normally call out the margin difference” [2].

What this genuinely establishes hasn’t changed since August: a three-year allocation of this size means STL has been qualified by a large AI-infrastructure buyer, and that qualification is real even though the customer is not named. What this edition adds is the mechanism. “Purchase orders released periodically” is framework language, not backlog language — it describes the ceiling on what STL is entitled to supply, not a schedule of what has been ordered. The distinction is not a technicality: a framework agreement can under-deliver against its headline value for reasons that have nothing to do with the underlying demand thesis being wrong — a slower customer ramp, a renegotiated allocation, a shift of volume to a second supplier under the same framework. None of that would necessarily show up as bad news; it would simply show up as the $1.11 billion not converting to revenue at the pace the headline implies, and there is no disclosed quarterly cadence against which to check that until STL reports data-centre revenue directly.

The last edition estimated this contract at roughly 54% of the then ₹18,618 crore order book, working from the same $1.11 billion figure. Because STL has not published a revised, segment-tagged order book since 30 June (§05), that proportion is carried forward for continuity here rather than re-confirmed against a fresh disclosure.

06 —

Capacity, Preform and the Fibre-Supply Question

Preform — STL makes its own

Asked directly, management confirmed STL manufactures its own glass preform rather than buying it in: “we’re amongst the very few companies in the world to have mastered the journey from glass to gigabit… from the purest grade of silicon, which we transform” [2]. That is a real point of differentiation against at least one Indian peer the desk’s shared evidence base has examined: Vindhya Telelinks sources its bare fibre under a long-term agreement with a JV rather than drawing its own preform, and states plainly that this leaves it “insulated to a significant extent from the bare fibre supply side risk” — a hedge STL, being vertically integrated, does not carry in the same way [19]. It also means STL is directly exposed to whatever is squeezing preform availability industry-wide, which the evidence base’s own primary source calls out explicitly: CRU Group reported in February 2026 that “glass preform has become a critical bottleneck in the value chain,” with Chinese suppliers running “near capacity limits” even where nominal spare capacity exists, because restarting idled lines takes months rather than weeks [19].

Utilisation, and “Lakshya”

Capacity utilisationNot quantified by management: “we don’t share, we don’t disclose actual numbers… capacity utilisation is improving quarter on quarter” [2]. The only figure found anywhere is a secondary report citing ~70% alongside the September expansion announcement [7] — not company-confirmed in this pass.
“Lakshya” — unveiled 3 Sep 2026MD Ankit Agarwal, at an investor meet: ~₹1,000 crore a year for three years (₹3,000 crore total, FY27–FY29), targeting ~50% more fibre and cable capacity; an FY29 revenue target of ₹20,000 crore against an FY26 base explicitly stated at ₹4,745 crore — roughly 4.2× in three years; and an EBITDA margin target reported at 27%+, up from ~13% in FY26 [7][30]. Agarwal’s own framing of the driver: “there are now more places where optical connectivity is required,” with fibre content per data-centre rack “increasing dramatically across successive hardware generations” [30].
Earlier capex framing (24 Jul call)A smaller, differently-scoped plan disclosed a month and a half earlier: ₹500 crore a year for three years (₹1,500 crore total) for “upgrades of our equipment and machines” and “de-bottlenecking from our current operations” [2]

This report treats the 24 July figure (₹1,500 Cr, equipment and de-bottlenecking) and the 3 September figure (₹3,000 Cr, new installed capacity) as two different things described at two different times, not a contradiction — but no source reviewed states explicitly whether the ₹1,500 Cr sits inside or alongside the ₹3,000 Cr, and this report will not assume either way. The FY29 revenue and margin targets are now corroborated across multiple outlets reporting the same 3 September investor meet, which is stronger sourcing than this report had for them previously, but this remains secondary press coverage of an investor meet, not STL’s own investor-day deck read directly [7][30].

On the company’s own stated FY26 base of ₹4,745 crore, the ₹20,000 crore FY29 target implies a revenue CAGR of roughly 61% sustained for three straight years. Q1 FY27’s 87% year-on-year growth shows that pace is achievable for one quarter off a depressed base; the target assumes something close to it holds for twelve, and — as §07 sets out — at least part of the quarters behind that 87% figure were also moving because of tariff relief, not only mix shift.

Realised pricing — the one number STL will not give

Asked directly about realised fibre and cable pricing, management declined: “we do not comment on a realisation,” adding only that STL holds “a healthy mix of good realisation from data centre as well as the telecom side” [2]. This is one of the two most consequential non-disclosures in this report (the other is in §07), because it is a number that would help settle the question the user asked this desk to test. The desk’s shared evidence base independently documents a real, sharp move in global bare-fibre pricing — China G.652.D bare fibre up 418% year-on-year and Europe up 159% to March 2026, per a UBS note citing CRU data [19][20] — but that verdict is explicitly qualified as “PARTLY HOLDS, with a timing catch”: Birla Cable’s own FY26 (April 2025–March 2026) OFC segment revenue declined, with management calling it “prolonged weakness…both domestically and globally,” and the same company’s own text says the super-cycle pricing environment only “began” in FY2026-27 — i.e. from April 2026 [19]. STL’s own quarterly numbers in §09 line up with that shape in part — but §07 shows that shape also lines up with a US tariff timeline that has nothing to do with fibre scarcity. What STL will not confirm is whether Q1 FY27’s margin jump reflects it actually capturing higher $/km on the fibre and cable it sells, whether it reflects tariff relief, or some mix of both with operating leverage layered on top — and those are three different stories that would look identical in a single reported margin number.

The 760-basis-point Q3 FY26 figure is sourced to a secondary earnings-call-highlights aggregator, not independently cross-checked against STL’s own filed Q3 FY26 transcript in this pass [26]. Adding it back to the reported 10.3% gives an illustrative “ex-tariff” margin of roughly 17.9% for that quarter — this report’s own arithmetic, offered only to show the scale of the swing, not as a restated figure STL has published.

This is the finding the last edition did not have, and it complicates the mix-shift story rather than replacing it. STL’s own framing of Q1 FY27 — the quarter this whole thesis rests on — is improved product mix and operating leverage from AI data-centre demand [1][2]. That is a real mechanism and this report does not dispute it. But the same three quarters also trace almost exactly the US tariff relief timeline: a large, quantified tariff drag in Q3 FY26, a partial-quarter easing in Q4 FY26, and a full quarter of the lower rate in Q1 FY27. STL has not disclosed how much of the Q3-to-Q1 margin recovery is tariff relief versus mix shift versus operating leverage, and did not raise tariffs at all on the 24 July Q1 FY27 call this report could source [2] — a silence that reads differently once the size of the Q3 FY26 swing is known.

The live risk this creates

CRISIL’s 16 June 2026 rating rationale — the most current primary document this report could open — names US tariffs as “a key monitorable,” noting that while the February 2026 reduction brought the rate to 10%, a proposed additional 12.5% tariff on India creates uncertainty [25]. This report could not independently corroborate the 12.5% figure or its status beyond that one rating-agency mention, and states it exactly that cautiously: a single, current, primary-adjacent source names a specific, material, unresolved tariff risk, and this report has not been able to check it against a second one.

North America revenue share54% of Q1 FY27 revenue, up from 39% in FY26 [2] — more than half the business now sits behind this exact policy question, up from under two-fifths a year ago
Local manufacturingAn existing “Build America, Buy America” (BABA)-compliant plant in Lugoff, South Carolina, established to qualify for BEAD-funded projects [23]; part of management’s own tariff mitigation is using this facility “more effectively” [26]. Separately, the 24 July call flagged a further US$100 million investment over five years for connectivity products serving both telecom and data-centre customers — not yet finalised: “once we’re able to finalize and detail out the investment…then we’ll be able to share that” [2]
BEAD programmeFramed only qualitatively on the Q1 FY27 call — “more than 140 million homes will be served by fiber by 2030, supported by large government programs like BEAD” [2] — with no quantified near-term STL revenue contribution. The desk’s evidence base independently confirms BEAD reached all-56-state Final Proposal approval only by 25 August 2026, with construction still described by NTIA as “just beginning” and peak construction activity not expected until 2027–28 per other reporting [19].

The honest read: tariffs were a large, real, quantified drag on STL as recently as Q3 FY26, have since eased, and remain a live and asymmetric risk the company itself (via its rating agency) still flags. That is a materially different picture from “no discussion of tariffs on the most recent call,” which is technically true of the Q1 FY27 transcript alone but understates how much tariffs have actually driven the numbers this report is otherwise crediting to the AI story. BEAD, separately, is real and probably still years from its volume peak rather than a present tailwind.

The demerger — what STL kept and what it gave up

STL demerged its Global Services Business into STL Networks Limited, trading as Invenia, effective 31 March 2025, with shareholders receiving one STL Networks share for every STL share held [12][13]. What went: systems integration and managed services across network, data-centre, cloud and cybersecurity domains for telecom, enterprise and government clients. What stayed: optical networking, optical connectivity, data centres and enterprise solutions — the entire business this report is about — across 100+ countries and 10+ manufacturing facilities [13]. The only financials this report could find for the demerged entity are a pre-separation FY25 baseline from the desk’s evidence base — revenue ~₹1,180 crore, EBITDA ~₹81 crore, order book >₹4,249 crore [19] — which describes the carved-out segment before it began trading independently, not a like-for-like current result. No FY26 or Q1 FY27 actuals for STL Networks were located in this research pass either, a gap this report is flagging rather than filling with the FY25 number.

Promoter holding, the QIP, and pledges

Promoter holding sat essentially flat around 44.1–44.5% from December 2024 through March 2026 [16]. The June 2026 quarter — which closed the same day the QIP allotment was finalised — is where sources diverge: one shareholding aggregator shows 44.51% promoter / 55.49% public, a different one shows a fuller breakdown (Promoters 42.29%, Mutual Funds 11.33%, Insurance 0.80%, FIIs 19.71%, DIIs 1.14%, Retail 24.73%, summing to 100%). The two do not agree, and this report prefers 42.29%: the QIP added 2.5728 crore shares, all to named institutional investors and none to the promoter group [11], which on a pre-QIP base of roughly 48.82 crore shares at 44.44% promoter holding arithmetically dilutes to almost exactly 42.3% on the enlarged 51.39 crore-share base — the reconciling figure, shown as derived arithmetic rather than an independently confirmed filing. No pledge of promoter shares was found in any source checked for the current period; this is an absence of evidence rather than a confirmed zero, and should be verified against the company’s own shareholding-pattern filing before being relied on.

The test this edition was asked to run comes down to one comparison. Since 27 August, STL has added one $288 million contract (§04) and a ₹3,000 crore “Lakshya” capacity-and-targets announcement (§06) to the public record. Over the same window its market capitalisation rose by roughly ₹5,242 crore (₹37,007 Cr to ₹42,249 Cr) [21][22]. The single new, quantified fact (the $288 million deal, ~₹2,400–2,750 Cr) accounts for well under half of the market-cap increase it accompanied, and the Lakshya announcement is a target and a promise to spend money, not a new source of confirmed revenue. On the company’s own, still-unrevised order book, still-undisclosed realised pricing, a FY29 revenue target that implies a ~61% three-year CAGR from FY26’s ₹4,745 crore base, and a margin recovery this edition can now show is entangled with tariff relief rather than cleanly attributable to an AI-fibre shortage, the price has moved by more than the disclosed facts have, for a second consecutive edition.

Scenarios

Bull
Both allocation agreements convert at or near their headline pace, the $100mn+ Neuralis orders keep arriving, and the Lakshya capacity plan comes on stream in time to supply them without STL losing share to HFCL, Corning, Prysmian or Fujikura’s own expansions. STL updates the order book with a materially higher, segment-tagged figure at the Q2 FY27 print, realised pricing (once finally disclosed) confirms the company is capturing the CRU-documented spike rather than just running the plant harder or riding tariff relief, and the ₹20,000 Cr / 27%+ FY29 targets prove to be guidance rather than aspiration. The proposed additional 12.5% India tariff does not materialise. The ~2.27× order-book multiple normalises as the denominator, not just the price, starts moving.
Base
Growth decelerates from Q1’s 87% as the comparison base normalises, and the two new allocation deals convert on a schedule closer to steady drawdown than front-loaded delivery. Margin holds near 20–21% rather than reaching the guided 23%, as the tariff-relief tailwind that helped get it there stops adding further quarter-on-quarter benefit even if it doesn’t reverse. The order book is updated at Q2 but by less than the announced deal values might suggest, because “allocation” and “order book” are not the same accounting concept. The stock digests the 750%-plus move over several quarters while the reporting catches up to the news flow.
Bear
The proposed additional 12.5% India tariff CRISIL flags is imposed, reopening a version of the ~760bps Q3 FY26 margin hit just as the market has re-rated the stock on the assumption that quarter is safely in the past. Separately or additionally, industry-wide capacity additions (HFCL, Birla Cable/VTL, Corning-NVIDIA, Prysmian’s $1bn+ US build, Fujikura) arrive into the same demand pool STL is counting on and compress the pricing STL will not itself disclose. A quarter that misses the raised 23% margin guide, on a stock that is roughly 86% above its 200-DMA with a margin recovery this report can no longer describe as purely structural, has no valuation or technical floor nearby to arrest it.

Key Risks

01
Part of the margin recovery may be tariff relief, not mix shift — and it can reverse
A ~760bps US-tariff hit to EBITDA margin in Q3 FY26 eased after a February 2026 rate cut to 10%, tracking almost exactly the same three quarters (10.3% → 15.1% → 20.8%) STL and the last edition credited to AI mix shift. CRISIL’s own June 2026 rationale names a proposed additional 12.5% India tariff as a live, unresolved monitorable. STL has not disclosed how much of the recovery is which mechanism.
02
Both new hyperscaler deals are allocations, not backlog
The $1.11bn and $288mn contracts both describe a ceiling on future purchase orders released periodically, not a fixed schedule of revenue. Either can under-deliver against its headline value for reasons that have nothing to do with AI data-centre demand being wrong — a slower ramp, a renegotiated share, volume shifting to another qualified supplier — and none of that would necessarily be disclosed as bad news.
03
The order book is now eleven-plus weeks stale
₹18,618 Cr as of 30 June 2026, unrevised through a second hyperscaler signing, the Lakshya announcement and a fresh all-time high. Market cap/order book has widened to ~2.27× from 1.99× purely on the price move, with the one number that would confirm new business — an updated book — not yet published.
04
Realised pricing is undisclosed
Management explicitly declined to discuss realisation. Whether Q1 FY27’s margin jump reflects STL capturing the CRU-documented fibre-price spike, tariff relief, or a mix-and-leverage effect — or some combination — cannot be independently verified from anything STL itself has published.
05
Extreme technical extension, tested and rejected once already
+867.85% from the 52-week low, +564.66% over one year, and roughly 86% above the 200-DMA. The stock has already touched an all-time high (₹912) and pulled back 10.2% from it on below-average volume. No support inside the levels §08 generates.
06
LTASM Stage IV surveillance, unchanged
150 days as of the 18 September scan — the most severe long-term surveillance stage, carried unchanged since the last edition. Surveillance-listed scrips typically face raised margin requirements and routine exclusion from broker MTF lists; confirm eligibility with your broker.
07
Everyone is expanding capacity at once
HFCL is adding optical fibre and cable capacity, Birla Cable/Vindhya Telelinks describe record pricing attracting new supply, Corning and NVIDIA are expanding US connectivity capacity 10×, and Prysmian and Fujikura have separately announced $1bn-plus and up to $1.6bn of new capacity. Optical fibre has a long history of capacity arriving quickly once margins turn attractive — which is the industry’s own current, self-reported condition [19].
08
Capacity-funding and execution risk
The ₹3,000 Cr Lakshya expansion is to be funded through accruals and/or debt on a balance sheet that only reached net cash in July via a ₹1,500 Cr QIP. A separate, smaller ₹1,500 Cr capex figure was given on the July call for equipment and de-bottlenecking; whether it sits inside or alongside the ₹3,000 Cr is not stated in the sources reviewed.
09
One quarter at the new level, still
Q1 FY27 remains the only quarter carrying the fully-recovered turnaround. The preceding quarters were near-breakeven, loss-making, or tariff-depressed (§09). Q2 FY27 is the first real test of repeatability at this level and has not yet reported.
10
The Prysmian verdict is on appeal and unresolved
A US jury awarded Prysmian $96.5 million against STL’s US subsidiary in August 2024 for trade-secret violations; a District Court upheld the verdict on 29 August 2025. STL filed an appeal in September 2025, and CRISIL’s 16 June 2026 rationale describes the matter as still “sub judice” and under close monitoring — i.e. unresolved as of the most current primary source this report could open.
13 —

Summary

RATING: HOLD — TURNAROUND REAL, BUT THE ORDER BOOK HASN’T CAUGHT UP TO THE PRICE

Nothing in this edition contradicts the business case the last one made. STL manufactures its own preform, has now been qualified by at least one and probably two separate hyperscale AI-infrastructure buyers, closed a ₹1,500 crore QIP that took it to net cash, and has CRISIL’s rating outlook moving from Negative (October 2025) to Stable (June 2026) as its balance sheet improved — net leverage down to 1.8× from 3.2×, interest cover up to 2.9× from 1.9×. All of that is real.

What this edition adds is more complicated than what it confirms. A second hyperscaler contract, a named “Lakshya” plan (₹20,000 Cr FY29 revenue, 27%+ margin, ₹3,000 Cr of capex) and a 13.7% price advance sit against an order book that has not moved since 30 June, realised pricing the company will not disclose, two contracts that are both, on the company’s own language, allocations rather than firm backlog — and a tariff history this edition traced for the first time, showing a ~760bps US-tariff hit to margin in Q3 FY26 that eased on a February 2026 rate cut across almost exactly the same quarters STL credits to AI mix shift. CRISIL itself still flags a proposed further India tariff as unresolved. Market cap against the unchanged order book has widened from 1.99× to roughly 2.27× purely on price.

The desk’s own shared research into the AI-data-centre and optical-fibre theme concludes that the fibre-supply story “PARTLY HOLDS, with a timing catch” — strong and real from roughly April 2026, but not the two-year trend the headlines imply. This edition adds a second reason for caution alongside it: even the part of STL’s own margin recovery that looks most like proof of the fibre-shortage story is, on the evidence gathered here, at least partly a tariff story instead. The turnaround in the P&L is genuine either way. Whether it is the AI-fibre shortage specifically that earns the current price — rather than a temporary tariff tailwind stacked on top of a real but smaller structural improvement — still cannot be answered from STL’s own disclosures, because the company will not break out realised pricing, and because the one call where tariffs might have been discussed against the current run-rate did not mention them. Until an updated order book, a second profitable quarter, and some clarity on the tariff-versus-mix split arrive together, this remains a name whose facts have improved and whose price has improved faster.
