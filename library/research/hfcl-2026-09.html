How Much Of This Is Actually Data-Centre?

This is the question this update exists to answer, and HFCL’s own disclosure gives a specific, checkable number — a much smaller one than the surrounding narrative implies.

Both FY27 target figures (“more than ₹700 crore” and “₹800 crore”) trace to the same Q1 FY27 disclosure window; this report could not determine which is the more precise or final number and shows both [7][8]. The FY27 revenue base is FY26’s ₹4,949.27 Cr grown at the guided +40%, which is itself guidance rather than a result — the percentage is derived and labelled as such.

Put plainly: on management’s own reporting, the specifically-labelled “data-centre connectivity” line was about one-twentieth of Q1 FY27 revenue and is targeted to reach roughly one-tenth of FY27 revenue. The remaining ~88–90% of the growth story — the ~₹16,000 Cr optical fibre cable book, the export surge from 24% to 55.5% of revenue, the BharatNet and domestic telecom orders — is not specifically attributed to AI or hyperscale demand in HFCL’s own disclosure, even though management repeatedly invokes that demand as a driver: Nahata told analysts of “huge demand for optical fibre cables in the data centre segment, especially in the US,” and separately that “the emergence of artificial intelligence, hyperscale data centers, cloud computing…is creating an entirely new source of demand for advanced optical fiber infrastructure” [4][7]. Those statements describe a demand tailwind for the generic OFC business broadly, which is a defensible and evidence-consistent claim — but they are not the same claim as “X rupees of our order book is data-centre business,” and management did not quantify the latter beyond the ~₹100 Cr / ₹700–800 Cr figures above.

This desk’s own shared evidence base on the AI data-centre supply chain reaches the same conclusion from the other direction. Its verdict on optical fibre and cable is that the theme “PARTLY HOLDS, with a timing catch”: the 2026 pricing and demand picture is real, but at the India cable-maker level even FY26 was not uniformly strong, and the strongest evidence is recent and still building rather than a two-year trend [4]. More pointedly, that evidence base’s verdict on optical interconnect — the acute, well-documented global shortage of indium-phosphide lasers and 800G/1.6T transceivers that dominates AI-infrastructure commentary — explicitly fails for HFCL and every other company this desk covers in the space: none of them make transceivers, lasers or InP substrates. HFCL’s “Data Centre Interconnect” push, run through its subsidiary HTL Limited, is on the evidence gathered a fibre, cable and passive-connectivity business, not an active optical-component manufacturer [4]. That distinction matters: the component of the AI buildout with the most acute, well-priced shortage (lasers and transceivers) is not one HFCL participates in. What HFCL sells into data centres is glass fibre and cable — a real and growing market, but a different, lower-value layer of the stack than the one generating the most dramatic shortage headlines.

Figures are shown exactly as each source states them [4][7][9][10]; this report could not fully reconcile the arithmetic bridging the December-2026 milestone to the cumulative post-expansion totals across the several board approvals disclosed through 2026, and has not attempted to re-derive a bridge that isn’t in the sources. The preform facility’s completion date is itself given two ways — October 2028 in one source and July 2029 in another — and both are shown rather than one being silently picked.

HFCL already makes some of its own preform (~300 MT/year, via existing facilities) and is doubling that capacity through a new greenfield plant under subsidiary HFCL Technologies Private Limited [9][10]. This is a genuinely relevant fact against this desk’s evidence base, which documents glass preform as “a critical bottleneck in the value chain” globally, with some fibre producers lacking integrated preform capability at all [4]. HFCL’s position is better than a pure cable-maker that sources fibre externally (Vindhya Telelinks, for instance, explicitly relies on a supply agreement with a joint-venture fibre producer rather than making its own [4]) — but at 300→600 MT/year, HFCL’s own preform capacity remains a fraction of what 43–62 million fibre-km of annual fibre and cable output implies in raw-material terms, so full backward integration is still years away even after the new plant is built.

The demand pressure is not in dispute: Nahata told analysts “If I say yes to one, I have to say no to other one. That has become the situation” [7] — capacity-constrained, by his own account, though management gave no percentage utilisation figure on the call, and none was found elsewhere in this research. On the order-book arithmetic alone, ₹26,665 crore against even the most generous post-expansion capacity figures confirms the August edition’s core point: the constraint on HFCL is how fast fibre and cable can physically be made, not whether demand exists — and that constraint has, if anything, tightened since August, since the order book has kept growing (§2, §3) while the capacity targets that would service it are dated out to mid-to-late 2028.

06 —

Exports, Pricing and the Tariff Crosscurrents

Export revenue was ₹1,063.30 crore in Q1 FY27, 55.53% of total revenue, up from ₹209.70 crore (24.08%) in Q1 FY26 — a 405.7% year-on-year increase [7]. That transformation, already the centrepiece of the August edition, is unchanged and unrevised this month; no new export-revenue figure has been reported since, only new order announcements (§2).

On tariffs, HFCL is currently a net beneficiary of US trade policy toward China, not a target of it — in management’s own words. Nahata told the Q1 FY27 call: “U.S. is the largest market…there is an administrative advice that administration is not in favor of buying from China…Today, there is a 35% duty on Chinese fiber” [7]. He also disclosed a past episode the other way: “When there was 50% duty imposition…Customers helped me, and they were very helpful. When the duty has been refunded, we have paid them back” [7] — describing a US tariff HFCL itself was subject to at some point, which was later refunded; the exact tariff regime and dates behind that comment were not identified in this research. The net picture from HFCL’s own disclosure is two-sided: it has faced (and had refunded) a US duty of its own, while currently benefiting from a 35% US duty that specifically disadvantages Chinese fibre.

The reverse trade dynamic is also live: China’s Ministry of Commerce extended anti-dumping duties of 7.4%–30.6% on Indian single-mode optical fibre for a further five years from 14 August 2026, following a review requested by China’s own domestic industry — which this desk’s evidence base reads as implicit evidence that Indian (and by extension HFCL’s) fibre exports have become large enough to warrant Chinese protectionism [4]. HFCL is, in other words, positioned on the winning side of the US-China tariff dynamic and the losing side of a China-India one, in the same period.

On pricing, this desk’s evidence base carries CRU/UBS bare-fibre data (via a two-level aggregator chain, flagged accordingly) showing China G.652.D fibre up 165% since January 2026 and 418% year-on-year to CNY83.40/fibre-km as of 31 March 2026, and the data-centre-oriented G.657.A1 grade up 130% (Europe) and 69% (US) over the same January–March window [5]. No India- or HFCL-specific $/km realised price was found in this research, so this report cannot say how much of HFCL’s own margin expansion (§9) reflects pass-through of this pricing move versus volume and mix. The timing is worth holding in mind: the evidence base’s own reading of Birla Cable’s FY26 annual report is that the sector-wide pricing “super-cycle” is described by at least one Indian cable-maker as having only begun in FY2026-27 (from April 2026) [4] — whereas HFCL’s own quarterly recovery was already well underway by Q2 FY26 (September 2025), per the trend in §9. That suggests HFCL’s initial turnaround (Q2–Q4 FY26) owed more to volume, mix and execution than to the fibre-pricing spike, while the sharper Q1 FY27 acceleration (+119.9% YoY) coincides with the period the pricing environment itself is described as having turned.

Only the ₹77 Cr FY26 figure is an actual; everything else is a management target, and the FY27–FY29 figures have themselves moved upward within a matter of weeks as the Defsys acquisition was folded into the base — itself a sign of how much of the “bigger” defence story is inorganic.

The June 2026 restructuring is real and substantive: HFCL Advance Systems Private Limited (HASPL) now holds an 80% stake in Raddef (radar and surveillance), 100% of HFCL Defence Systems, the thermal-weapon-sight business, and the aerostructure business of Defsys Solutions, the last of which is the source of the ~₹1,890 crore export order book management now cites [11]. That order book was won by Defsys before HFCL acquired it — it is purchased pipeline, not evidence that HFCL itself is winning new defence orders at a faster rate. Separately, HFCL has approved a ₹230 crore ammunition plant in Andhra Pradesh (multi-mode hand grenades, ~40 lakh units/year capacity), targeted to start production by December 2027 [11] — a genuine greenfield commitment, though one still years from revenue.

The arithmetic discipline from the August edition still applies, updated for the new numbers: a defence-and-aerospace order book of roughly ₹2,300 crore (organic plus acquired) against a FY29 revenue target of ₹5,000 crore requires either the acquired book to convert unusually fast, or several more years of order wins that have not yet happened. Treat the FY28/29 defence numbers as targets on an enlarged but still mostly-inherited base, not as a book that is already won.

Two margin numbers, one quarter

Screener’s own operating-margin construction (excluding other income) shows 22% for Q1 FY27 and 3.3% for Q1 FY26 [12]; the company’s own reported EBITDA margin for the same quarters is 23.25% and 4.93% [3][7]. As with the desk’s other reports that have hit this same gap, the two are not in conflict — they are different constructions (the ~15–31 crore difference each quarter is consistent with an other-income component the screener metric excludes), and both show the same swing from a barely-profitable to a strongly profitable quarter. Neither figure is wrong; quoting one against the other without naming the difference would be.

Promoters, pledge and dilution

Promoter holding was 28.29% at 30 June 2026, down from 37.84% at 30 September 2023 — a roughly 9.5 percentage-point decline over about three years [12][22]. Against that trend, promoters have put fresh money in: two promoter entities, NextWave Communications Private Limited and Satellite Finance Private Limited, were allotted ₹555 crore of warrants (7.5 crore warrants at ₹74/share, 25% paid upfront), exercisable within 18 months of the 24 April 2026 EGM approval [9]. Fully converted, that is roughly a low-single-digit percentage of the current share count (derived, approximate — the exact pre-warrant base could not be pinned down precisely). This report could not confirm HFCL’s current promoter pledge percentage from the sources checked; one aggregator states no change in pledged shares as of March 2026 without giving an absolute figure, and a separately circulated ~54% figure appears to be stale, referencing an earlier period. Promoter shares that had been pledged were released in full in November 2021, per contemporaneous reporting [23], but that is a five-year-old data point and this report will not carry it forward as current.

The rating round-trip

CARE Ratings downgraded HFCL to A-/Stable (long-term) and A2+ (short-term) on 29 August 2025, citing a 9% revenue decline in FY25, a 24.8% revenue decline in Q1 FY26, a 49% fall in FY25 profit, and net debt/EBITDA of 4.85x [16]. Less than a year later, on 1 July 2026, CARE upgraded HFCL’s long-term facilities to A+, outlook Positive, and short-term facilities to A1 [15]. The credit story and the equity story have moved in the same direction and on a similar timeline — both troughed around mid-to-late 2025 and both have re-rated through 2026. ICRA, separately, withdrew its own HFCL rating in April 2026 under its standard withdrawal policy; this report found no stated reason and does not assume one [17].

Scenarios

Bull
The order book keeps growing past ₹26,665 Cr as the 1 September ₹2,329 Cr order and further export wins are confirmed and disclosed with more clarity on composition. Capacity additions land on the stated dates, easing the constraint Nahata described. Data-centre connectivity revenue scales toward and past the ₹700–800 Cr FY27 target, giving the AI narrative a growing, disclosed number to point to rather than a small one. The Defsys and Raddef defence assets begin converting acquired pipeline into reported HFCL revenue, and operating cash flow turns positive as the Q4 FY26 unbilled-revenue pattern does not repeat. The credit upgrade continues, and the technical picture recovers back above the 50-DMA.
Base
Growth decelerates toward the guided +40% as the extraordinary Q1 FY27 comparison base normalises, consistent with management’s own signalling. The data-centre-specific line grows in absolute terms but stays a minority contributor to revenue for several more years, exactly as its current ~5–12% share implies. The order book keeps growing steadily from new export and BharatNet awards without another contract on the scale of the US$1.1bn deal. Cash conversion improves gradually as billing catches up, but debtor days stay elevated given the BharatNet-heavy mix. The stock consolidates the past year’s move while the ASM stage and the moving-average structure work themselves out one way or the other.
Bear
The recent pullback and the crack below the 50-DMA prove to be the start of a larger de-rating rather than a pause, amplified by LT-ASM Stage IV margin requirements. Global fibre pricing, which this desk’s evidence base already flags as recent and still-forming rather than a settled multi-year trend, softens as capacity is added industry-wide (Corning, Prysmian and Fujikura are all separately expanding). The data-centre-specific revenue line disappoints against its own ₹700–800 Cr target, undercutting the part of the story genuinely tied to AI demand. Negative operating cash flow persists into FY27, receivables continue to build against BharatNet and other government-linked work, and the Defsys/Raddef acquisitions prove harder to integrate or monetise than the order-book headline suggests. A 57.6× trailing multiple with one strong quarter behind it has little cushion against any of this.
12 —

Key Risks

01
The AI/data-centre share is much smaller than the story around it
Disclosed data-centre connectivity revenue was ~₹100 Cr of ₹1,915 Cr in Q1 FY27 (~5%), targeted at ~₹700–800 Cr of a guided ~₹6,929 Cr FY27 (~10–12%). Most of the order book and the export surge is generic OFC/telecom demand, not disclosed AI-specific business. A price built on the AI framing is a price built on a small, if growing, slice of the disclosed numbers.
02
The framework has cracked for the first time
Price sits fractionally below the 50-DMA after 22 weeks in Stage 2, coinciding with an escalation to the most severe Long-Term ASM stage and a 13.9% fall since the last edition, on record that reads as profit-booking after a six-month, 233%+ run rather than any single adverse disclosure [26][27]. When momentum and fundamentals diverge, this desk’s own house rule is to lead with the disagreement, not the order book.
03
Order-book composition is partly undisclosed
Management has not quantified the order book by BharatNet, domestic telecom, export or data-centre customer type, and it is not clear whether the ~₹2,300 Cr defence figure (including the proposed Defsys acquisition) sits inside or beside the ₹26,665 Cr headline total. Circulating BharatNet order figures (₹2,666 Cr, ₹1,244 Cr and others) could not be reconciled against each other or against the headline total.
04
Negative operating cash flow in the record-profit year
FY26 operating cash flow was −₹378 Cr and free cash flow −₹723 Cr, against a reported PAT of ₹329 Cr and debtor days of 163. The gap was funded by fresh borrowings and promoter warrant proceeds, not by the business itself. This is precisely the BharatNet/government-receivables risk this report was asked to test, and the numbers support treating it as live.
05
Defence order-book growth is mostly acquired, not won
The ~₹1,890 Cr export order book behind the enlarged defence figure was won by Defsys Solutions before HFCL acquired the business. FY28/29 revenue targets (₹3,000 Cr+, then ₹5,000 Cr) still require several more years of order wins on top of an inherited book roughly a fifth to a third of that size.
06
Concentration in a handful of large export contracts
The US$1.1bn contract remains roughly 38% of the order book on unchanged terms. Layered on top are several discrete export orders (₹441.53 Cr, ₹495.80 Cr, ₹522.73 Cr, ~₹2,329 Cr) whose customers are mostly undisclosed — a small number of counterparties carry a large share of forward visibility.
07
Capacity still trails the order book, on conflicting timelines
Even the most generous post-expansion capacity figures (43.10M fkm fibre, 62M fkm cable) are dated to mid-to-late 2028, while the order book already stands at 5.4× FY26 revenue and keeps growing. The preform facility’s own completion date is given as both October 2028 and July 2029 across sources.
08
Promoter dilution via warrants, against a multi-year holding decline
Promoter holding fell from 37.84% (Sep 2023) to 28.29% (Jun 2026). The ₹555 Cr warrant issue to two promoter entities at ₹74/share is a large discount to the current price and is dilutive to other shareholders on conversion, even as it is a genuine capital commitment from the promoter group. Current pledge status could not be confirmed from the sources checked.
09
Global fibre-pricing cyclicality
This desk’s evidence base already flags the fibre pricing “super-cycle” as recent (from around April 2026) rather than a settled multi-year trend, with major producers (Corning, Prysmian, Fujikura) all expanding capacity in response to the same pricing signal that is helping HFCL today. Industry capacity has historically been added quickly when margins are attractive.
10
Long-Term ASM Stage IV surveillance
The most severe surveillance stage, escalated since the August edition. Typically carries materially higher margin requirements and routine exclusion from broker margin-funding (MTF) approved lists. Confirm eligibility with your broker before assuming this can be held on margin.
13 —

Summary

RATING: HOLD — THE TURNAROUND IS REAL, THE AI DATA-CENTRE SLICE IS STILL SMALL

The business case has, if anything, strengthened since August: the order book has grown past ₹26,665 crore with a further ~₹2,329 crore order since, the defence platform has been meaningfully expanded (even if mostly by acquisition), a preform plant narrows one real supply-chain gap, and CARE Ratings has completed a round-trip from downgrade to upgrade that mirrors the equity story almost exactly. None of that is contradicted here, and none of it is new cause for doubt.

What this update adds is a number the August edition did not have: how much of the story is actually data-centre. On management’s own disclosure, data-centre connectivity revenue was about ₹100 crore of a ₹1,915 crore quarter — roughly 5% — and is targeted to reach ₹700–800 crore of FY27 revenue, roughly 10–12%. That is a real and growing business, and this desk’s own evidence base confirms the macro demand behind it is genuine. It is also a small fraction of the ₹26,665 crore order book and the 55.5%-of-revenue export business that the AI-data-centre narrative gets attached to in commentary and, at points, in management’s own framing. Most of what has re-rated this stock is a conventional, well-executed optical-fibre-cable and telecom-equipment turnaround with real export and BharatNet demand behind it — not an AI data-centre business in the numbers that HFCL itself reports. That is the sense in which the story runs ahead of the evidence: not because the turnaround is fake, but because the specific AI framing carries more of the valuation than the disclosed AI-specific revenue can yet support.

Two further facts earn this a Hold rather than either extreme. The price has already fallen 13.9% since the last edition and cracked marginally below its 50-DMA for the first time, under the most severe ASM stage — the market has started to ask some of the same questions this report asks, independent of this desk’s analysis. And operating cash flow was negative in the very year profit turned positive, a receivables-and-billing pattern consistent with the BharatNet-heavy mix this report was asked to examine. Against that, market cap to order book at 1.24× is cheaper than August’s 1.44×, and cheaper than the closest large peer (Sterlite Technologies) on every multiple shown in §10.

The order book supports a genuine business. The pricing does not yet need the AI data-centre story to be as large as it is currently made out to be — and by the company’s own figures, it isn’t, yet.
