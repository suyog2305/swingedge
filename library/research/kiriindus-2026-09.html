Why this matters more than the price move

A company holding more than ₹5,200 crore of net cash, with borrowings of roughly ₹42 crore, is proposing to sell equity-linked paper to raise ₹289 crore. Read that sentence next to the August thesis. The August bear case was that the market discounts this balance sheet because management intends to spend it on copper and fertilisers rather than return it. That was an argument about a stated intention. This is an action, and it points the same way.

On the reported terms, the issue dilutes the single asset that constitutes the entire investment case. The reason to look at this company at all is that it holds more cash per share than the share costs. An issue that raises the share count by more than it raises the cash makes that ratio worse, not better — for a company that does not, on any reading of its own balance sheet, need the money.

The fair reading of the price

₹475 is not evidence of impropriety and this report does not present it as such. SEBI’s preferential-pricing rules set a floor from volume-weighted averages over trailing 90-day and 10-day windows, and a strike struck in late August against a stock that closed at ₹493.30 on 27 August — and considerably lower through the preceding months — is consistent with that formula. What is notable is not the price but the timing and the direction: the paper was priced off a pre-rally market, and the rally arrived in the days after.

There is also a defensible corporate rationale, and it should be stated. Warrants at 25% upfront bring in a named strategic holder cheaply, and a ₹13,300 crore project programme benefits from anchor investors as much as from cash. That argument is about who owns the company, not about whether it can fund itself. On funding it plainly can, and the August question — what will management do with ₹5,200 crore — has now been answered once, and the answer was “raise more”.

Copper capacity is stated as 500,000 MTPA of refining by the company-sourced coverage and as 10 lakh tonnes in reporting on the clearance application; fertiliser as 1,050,000 MTPA plus phosphoric acid, or 16.5 lakh tonnes, on the same split. These are plausibly different scopes and different phases rather than contradictions, but the report cannot resolve them and does not pick one silently. The August characterisation survives every one of these numbers. A dyes and intermediates company is building a copper smelter and a fertiliser complex, at a programme cost roughly three and a half times its market capitalisation, funded with about three-quarters of the DyStar proceeds as equity and the rest with bank debt at 8.5–9%. Neither industry has any operational, technical or customer overlap with anything Kiri has run.

Two things have genuinely improved. Financial closure at over 95% means the debt is largely committed rather than hoped for, and a stated intent not to increase debt further bounds the leverage. And the equity component is roughly ₹4,000 crore rather than the full ₹5,200 crore — which leaves a residual, and it is that residual, not the whole pile, that is available for anything else. That is a materially smaller pot than the August framing implied, and it makes a large special dividend arithmetically harder, not easier.

A new risk the August report did not carry

The site has documented local opposition. Reporting on the environmental-clearance public hearing in Lunsapur village describes protests against the plant by residents of Rajula and Jafrabad talukas, with the hearing disrupted and detentions made. Environmental clearance has since been reported as received for both the copper and fertiliser projects. Clearance granted is not the same as land, water and community consent settled, and greenfield heavy industry on a Gujarat coastline is exactly where that distinction bites.

This is an arithmetic ceiling, not a forecast, and it will not be approached. It assumes Indian realisations track Chinese domestic spot one for one, full capture with no input-cost offset, full utilisation, and no captive consumption. All four assumptions are wrong in the same direction: Kiri consumes its own H-acid into vinyl sulphone and reactive dyes, so part of any rise is an internal transfer; Indian export realisations lag and discount Chinese spot; and input costs rise alongside. The purpose of the number is to bound the argument from above, not to size the outcome.

And it does change one August conclusion. The August report said the chemicals business was too small to matter against the cash, and used a doubling of operating profitability — about ₹64 crore annualised on the Q1 run rate (derived by annualising one quarter, which is a scaling and not a forecast) — as the illustration. On the ceiling above, that framing is too comfortable. With a genuine position in the world’s tightest dye intermediate, the operating line has room to move by a multiple of itself. What it does not have is room to reach ₹5,200 crore. Even an operating business earning four times the current run rate would produce roughly ₹254 crore of annual EBITDA (derived, on ₹15.90 Cr × 4 quarters × 4) against net cash of more than ₹5,200 crore. The cash still decides the outcome; the chemicals business is no longer irrelevant to it.

The timing gap is the same one the cluster has

Q1 FY27 ended on 30 June. H-acid’s step change came on 28 July. No reported Kiri number contains the squeeze, and none will until Q2 FY27 is published around November. What Q1 does show is the earlier part of the recovery arriving: standalone revenue of ₹295.3 crore against ₹202.1 crore, standalone EBITDA of ₹17.3 crore at a 5.86% margin, and a material margin of 31.9% against 23.5% a year earlier. That last figure is the realisation improvement showing up in the accounts, and it turned a loss-making operation profitable.

Consolidated Q1 FY27: revenue ₹312.36 Cr, operating EBITDA ₹15.90 Cr at 5.09%, other income ₹286 Cr, PAT approximately ₹290 Cr against ₹10.10 Cr a year earlier. The consolidated and standalone EBITDA figures differ slightly and both are shown.

An effective rate of roughly 4% in a year where non-operating income of ₹6,057 crore exceeded the entire profit before tax — the operating business ran a ₹221 crore loss — is a striking number, and it is the reason the “more than ₹5,200 crore net” figure should still carry a caveat. Either the receipt was largely shielded — by treaty relief, by the structure through which the Singapore proceeds were received, or by set-off against accumulated losses in a business that had been loss-making — or a liability remains outside the P&L. What can be said with confidence: ₹1,494 crore of contingent liabilities against a ₹3,834 crore market capitalisation is not a rounding item, and any assessment of net cash that ignores it is incomplete. The August caveat stands, better documented and slightly more uncomfortable.

Your system has re-admitted this name, and that is a real change with a real meaning. A negative enterprise value is still the headline, and it still deserves naming plainly. On the stated figures the market values Kiri’s entire operating business — plants, dyes franchise, intermediates capacity, a position in the world’s tightest reactive-dye intermediate — at less than zero. That was true in August at 62 paise on the rupee of cash and it is true today at 74.

What has changed is the price of the option, not the option. In August the argument was that a plan you disliked was already fully priced, and then some, and that any moderation of the plan was free. Twelve trading days later a fifth of that margin has been paid away, and the intervening evidence made the plan look firmer rather than softer: a ₹13,300 crore programme with financial closure over 95% complete, an equity call of roughly ₹4,000 crore against ₹5,200 crore of net proceeds, no dividend in the annual report, and a resolution to raise ₹289 crore more.

The valuation question is therefore unchanged in structure and worse in price. It will still be settled by board resolutions rather than by H-acid quotes. The difference is that one board resolution has now arrived, and it moved the case in the wrong direction while the multiple moved up.

Earnings are treasury, not operations
₹286 Cr of roughly ₹290 Cr of quarterly profit is other income — eighteen times the ₹15.90 Cr operating EBITDA. Site opposition and permitting at Jafrabad
Documented local protest in Rajula and Jafrabad talukas, Amreli district, against the copper refinery and fertiliser plant, including a disrupted environmental public hearing with detentions. Summary

RATING: HOLD — THE SYSTEM ADMITS IT; THE CASH QUESTION GOT HARDER

The rating does not change, and the reasoning behind it changes almost entirely. In August this desk rated Kiri Hold — The Cash Is Real, the Plan Is the Risk at ₹493.30, with the framework excluding the name and a 38% discount to stated net cash as compensation for a deployment plan nobody liked. Both halves of that sentence have now moved, in opposite directions, and they very nearly cancel.

What genuinely improved. Your own system has re-admitted this name. Relative strength went from 77 to 94 in a 1,617-stock universe, the trend template moved from FAILS to a full pass, and the provider’s list carries a fresh Stage 2 entry dated 22 August reading “Continues Trend”. The August report said explicitly that these were the conditions worth watching, and they have been met. The operating business is improving too: material margin moved from 23.5% to 31.9% year on year in Q1 FY27, turning a loss-making operation profitable, and Chinese H-acid has since gone from roughly CNY 40,000 per tonne at the start of 2026 to about CNY 100,000 by end-August — yuan, not rupees, which is the distinction that decides the magnitude. The copper programme is further advanced than August could establish, with financial closure over 95% and debt at 8.5–9%.

Why that is not enough to move the rating up. The pass is the thinnest the template can produce: one check clears its threshold by roughly half a percentage point, the moving-average cross is days old, and Stage 2 tenure is two weeks. And the framework cannot see the thing that matters here. On 31 August the board resolved to raise ₹288.92 crore by issuing warrants at ₹475, for shareholder approval on 29 September — from a company holding more than ₹5,200 crore of net cash and ₹42 crore of debt. On the reported terms it raises the share count by about 9.3% while raising the cash by about 5.6% (both derived): it dilutes the one asset the whole case rests on. Alongside it, the FY26 annual report filed 3 September declares no dividend, and the record shows none since 2020.

Why not Avoid. Because the arithmetic that made August interesting has not been destroyed, only reduced. The company is still debt-free, still holds more cash than its market value, still trades at an enterprise value below zero, and now also passes the momentum framework. Those are not the properties of a stock to walk away from. The discount to stated net cash is still roughly 26% (derived) and the discount to book is still among the widest in this library.

What the higher price does not change, and this is the sentence to keep. A share price is not a board resolution. Every risk the August report identified — more than 90% of a windfall pointed at industries the management has never run, a ₹13,300 crore programme at 3.5× the market capitalisation, an unconfirmed tax position, ₹1,494 crore of contingent liabilities, no distribution — is exactly where it was, and one of them got measurably worse in the fortnight the stock rose. The market has paid twelve paise more per rupee of cash for a cash pile that is now demonstrably less available to shareholders than it looked three weeks ago. That is a worse bargain than August, not a better one, and it is why a framework flip from fail to pass moves the analysis without moving the call.
