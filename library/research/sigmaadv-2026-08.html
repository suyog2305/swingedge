02 —

Read This First: You Are Not Looking at a Continuous Company

Megasoft Limited changed its name to Sigma Advanced Systems Limited after amalgamating Sigma Advanced Systems into itself. Ministry of Corporate Affairs approval came on 12 January 2026, exchange approvals on 3 February 2026, and the new symbol took effect on 9 February 2026.

This single fact governs how every historical number on this company should be read, and it is the reason this report leads with it rather than with the order book.

  • The listed entity has a long history; the business inside it does not. The share registry, listing date and legal continuity trace back to a company that was doing something entirely different. The aerospace and defence operation arrived through amalgamation.
  • Year-on-year comparisons are meaningless. Comparing Q1 FY27 to a prior-year quarter compares an aerospace manufacturer to whatever the predecessor entity was.
  • Multi-year charts are not one company’s price history. The +515% move from the 52-week low spans the period across which the identity changed.

This is not a criticism of the business. Reverse-merger and amalgamation routes to listing are legitimate and common in Indian aerospace and defence. It is a statement about what the data can and cannot tell you — and about why the usual screening metrics produce nonsense here.

The Q4 FY26 EBITDA figure is derived from the reported 11% sequential growth. Note that the company reports growth quarter on quarter rather than year on year — which, given Section 02, is the honest way to present it.

Two observations that matter more than the growth rate:

  • Revenue grew 16% but EBITDA only 11% — the operating margin compressed sequentially. The same pattern flagged at Rashi Peripherals, and it is worth watching for a second quarter.
  • “PAT from continuing operations” is a deliberate phrase. It implies discontinued operations exist — almost certainly the legacy Megasoft software business being wound down or divested. That is orderly, but it means the reported bottom line still contains transition effects.

Annualising Q1 revenue gives roughly ₹1,497 crore. Against a ₹14,758 crore market capitalisation, that is 9.9× annualised sales for a manufacturer.

An order book at more than five times annualised revenue is genuine, substantial visibility — comparable to HFCL and the strongest of the five watchlist names here. Whatever the complications in the financial history, this is a real number attached to real, named counterparties.

The composition tells you what kind of company this is. Over 90% international and 75–80% aerospace means Sigma is not an Indian defence-procurement play riding domestic budget cycles. It is a supplier into global aerospace supply chains, and the qualification barriers there are among the highest in manufacturing.

That cuts both ways, and the second edge deserves as much attention as the first: more than 90% international exposure means currency risk, foreign customer concentration, and dependence on global aerospace demand rather than Indian policy. It is the same structural concentration flagged at TD Power Systems, at a similar magnitude.

The Rolls-Royce Agreements

This is the substance of the story. Rolls-Royce is one of the world’s three major aero-engine manufacturers, and its supplier qualification process is among the most demanding in industry. Winning one long-term agreement is meaningful; winning a second, larger cumulative package months later is stronger evidence still — repeat business from a customer of that calibre is the clearest external validation a component manufacturer can get.

Combined, the two agreements represent roughly ₹5,400 crore, or about two-thirds of the entire order book, and the stock hit its upper circuit on the second announcement.

The concentration this creates should be stated plainly. A single customer accounting for something close to two-thirds of the book is a dependency, however prestigious that customer is. Long-term aerospace agreements are typically framework arrangements with volume commitments over many years — real, but with delivery schedules driven by the customer’s own build rates, which Sigma does not control.

The Artillery Order

Product155mm base bleed artillery shells
ValueUSD 104.9 million (~₹1,013 Cr)
TypeExport order

A ₹1,013 crore export order for artillery ammunition is roughly 12–13% of the order book and, importantly, sits in a completely different business from the Rolls-Royce aerospace work.

155mm ammunition is a genuinely large global market with elevated demand, and base-bleed shells are a higher-specification product than standard rounds. Winning an export order at this scale requires production capability and export clearance — both real barriers.

It also diversifies the book away from the aerospace concentration described in Section 06, which is the more valuable point. Watch whether defence ammunition becomes a repeatable second line or stays a single large win.

08 —

The UK and India Structure

Sigma operates a dual manufacturing footprint across India and the United Kingdom, with Bromford Precision Solutions as the named UK subsidiary through which the second Rolls-Royce agreement was secured.

The logic is straightforward and sound: a UK presence gives proximity, qualification standing and supply-chain credibility with European aerospace primes that an India-only manufacturer would struggle to establish, while Indian manufacturing provides the cost base. It is structurally the same play Jyoti CNC attempted with Huron.

That comparison is worth holding deliberately. Jyoti CNC’s European subsidiary is currently reducing group earnings and carries an unresolved export-control investigation. Cross-border manufacturing structures are harder to run than they are to describe. Sigma’s UK arm is currently winning business rather than dragging — but it is early, and this report has no visibility into segment-level UK profitability.

09 —

What This Report Cannot Tell You

Reports are more useful when they state their gaps. For Sigma, these are material:

  • Comparable historical financials. Because of the amalgamation, there is no clean multi-year record of this business as it currently exists.
  • The balance sheet. Debt, cash and working capital are not established in the sources reviewed. At 29.2× book with a large order book to fund, this is a significant gap.
  • Order book execution schedule. Whether ₹8,000 crore converts over three years or seven changes the annual revenue implication enormously.
  • Segment profitability. UK versus India, aerospace versus defence — none disclosed in what was reviewed.
  • The discontinued operations. What remains of the legacy business and what winding it down costs.

These are answerable from the company’s annual report and quarterly filings, and doing so would materially improve the quality of any decision here. This report does not estimate them.

The valuation measures disagree sharply, and which one you weight determines the conclusion.

On price to book at 29.2× and 9.9× annualised sales, this is the most expensive company in the exercise. On market cap to order book at 1.84×, it is reasonable — comparable to Man Industries at 1.58× and far better than TD Power at 10.6×.

The reconciliation is that Sigma has a large contracted future and a small realised present. The order book is five times current revenue, so the company is being valued on what it will deliver rather than on what it has delivered. That can be entirely correct — and it is exactly the situation where the execution schedule (Section 09) matters most, and is exactly what is not disclosed.

At 105% above its 200-DMA, there is also no technical reference anywhere near the current price. ₹600 (50-DMA) is 23% below; ₹379 (200-DMA) is 51% below.

13 —

Summary

RATING: HOLD — REAL ORDER BOOK, UNREADABLE FINANCIAL HISTORY

Sigma Advanced Systems has an order book of more than ₹8,000 crore — over five times annualised revenue — of which roughly ₹5,400 crore is Rolls-Royce long-term agreements and ₹1,013 crore is a 155mm artillery export order. Repeat business from Rolls-Royce is about as strong an external validation as an aerospace component manufacturer can obtain, and it is not in dispute.

Everything else about this company requires care. It was Megasoft Limited until 9 February 2026, and the amalgamation means there is no comparable financial history — the screener’s +7,084% sales growth is an artefact, not information. The balance sheet, the order-book execution schedule and segment profitability are all undisclosed in what was reviewed. It trades at 29.2× book, the highest multiple in this entire exercise, sits 105% above its 200-DMA after a 515% move, and carries LT-ASM Stage III surveillance.

The order book is real and the history is unreadable — both at once. That combination argues for treating this as a position sized to genuine uncertainty rather than to the strength of the Rolls-Royce headline, and for doing the filing-level work in Section 09 before treating it as anything more settled.
