What This Note Is, and How It Was Checked

A request reached this desk for a consolidated view of “the latest data around chip pricing, memory price increase, availability of data-centre raw materials like optic fibre, interconnect optics and other things,” naming seven Indian stocks: GNG Electronics (EBGNG), Sterlite Technologies (STLTECH), HFCL, Black Box (BBOX), STL Networks (STLNETWORK), Birla Cable (BIRLACABLE) and Vindhya Telelinks (VINDHYATEL). The last two are named separately because they trade separately today, even though they are in the process of becoming one listed entity. This note does not repeat that research. A desk researcher closed out a sourced evidence base on 19 September 2026 — findings with numbered sources, verdicts per leg, conflicts and gaps, and failed domains, cross-checked against two dated datapoint files the desk maintains — and over the two days that followed, the desk published a full report on each of the seven names [1]–[57]. This is the synthesis: what holds, what does not, and who actually captures it, written to agree with those six reports rather than to duplicate or contradict them.

Deceleration, and what it does not mean

Chaining the low ends of TrendForce’s own PC-relevant quarter-ahead calls — over 100% in Q1, 58–63% in Q2 (the conventional-DRAM figure; no separate PC figure exists for that quarter), 15–20% in Q3, 3–8% in Q4 [1][2][6] — implies contract prices near 3.7 times their end-2025 level by December 2026, on arithmetic mixing two related but not identical series and built on forecasts rather than reported prices. This is not this note’s own derivation; it is the same chained calculation the desk’s GNG report performs and flags with the identical caveats [52]. The rate of increase is decelerating; the level it is decelerating from is not falling. A quarter of +3–8% still compounds on top of a base that has already roughly tripled, and TrendForce itself revised the second half of 2026 upward, not downward, on 30 June [6] — the forecaster has so far been too cautious about how fast the rise fades, not too optimistic. The 9 July release’s own language for why the rate is slowing is affordability, not oversupply: “customers from consumer markets, such as PCs and smartphones, are reaching their affordability limit” [4][5].

Where this actually lands: GNG’s entry-level laptop

The sharpest downstream evidence in the whole record is GNG Electronics’ own account of what memory has done to an entry-level new laptop in India. Two figures for the starting point circulate: a Tradebrains report of the same call puts it at about ₹42,000, but GNG’s own filed transcript of its 30 July 2026 call gives the figure as “from INR40,000 to INR48,000” — the company’s own filed record is preferred here over the press paraphrase of it [20][21][52]. Management’s own words: memory costs “have gone up by 5–10% in the last quarter [Q1 FY27] while they have increased nearly 5 times since October 2025” [21]. This is the one point in the record where a forecast, a producer’s reported ASP change, and an Indian company’s own filed transcript all describe the same object from three different angles and agree on direction and rough scale.

04 —

Chips and Packaging

This leg holds, but narrowly, and it reaches none of the seven directly. TSMC’s CoWoS advanced-packaging wafer ASP is reportedly nearing 7nm-logic-level pricing, positioned by industry analysts as a growing profit driver in its own right, with monthly capacity ramping from roughly 75,000–80,000 wafers toward a 120,000–130,000-wafer target by end-2026 and OSAT partners adding a further 50,000–60,000 a month [47][48]. 2026 CoWoS demand is estimated near one million wafers against roughly 370,000 in 2024, with NVIDIA alone said to hold about 60% of that demand; the demand/supply gap is estimated at roughly 20% today, improving toward 10% into 2027 [48]. Advanced-packaging ASPs are reported rising 10–20% a year against only about 5% for ordinary logic wafers — this is a narrow, advanced-node, AI-specific price phenomenon, not broad-based chip inflation, and the sourcing here is trade-analyst synthesis rather than the primary-filing depth reached for memory [47][48].

Separately, GPU and passive-component lead times are genuinely stretched: Blackwell-class server GPU lead times are reported to have moved from 20–24 weeks to as much as 48 weeks with recurring 5–15% price increases, and MLCC (multi-layer ceramic capacitor) lead times of 20–52 weeks with roughly 30% across-the-board price increases from August 2026, attributed to AI server racks each consuming on the order of 40,000–60,000-plus MLCCs [49]. No India-specific 2026 chip-pricing action, for a foundry, controller or passive component, was found for any of the seven companies in this basket [49].

None of the seven is a chipmaker, foundry or OSAT. This leg’s relevance to the basket is indirect at best: through GNG Electronics’ memory input costs, already covered in full in §03, and through the generic “AI capex” and “hyperscale demand” language that HFCL and Black Box invoke in their own investor materials without being chip or packaging suppliers themselves [52]–[57]. Chip and packaging pricing belongs in this note because the user asked about it, not because it is a mechanism through which any of these seven earns money.
05 —

Optical Fibre and Cable

This is the leg closest to what four of the seven actually make or sell, and it is also the one carrying the sharpest timing catch in the whole evidence base.

The price moves, and the bottleneck behind them

CRU Group reported on 26 February 2026 that China’s G.652.D bare-fibre price moved above Europe’s for the first time in over seven years, and that “glass preform has become a critical bottleneck in the value chain,” with Chinese suppliers running “near capacity limits” despite nominal spare capacity because restarting idled lines “requires time, technical preparation and capital commitment” measured in months, not weeks [29]. A UBS note relayed via BigGo Finance on 2 April 2026, citing the same CRU data, put China G.652.D bare fibre at roughly $12.07 per fibre-km in March 2026, up 165% since January 2026 and 418% year on year; Europe’s equivalent grade was up 159% year on year to roughly $9.10; the data-centre-oriented G.657.A1 grade rose 130% in Europe and 69% in the US over the same January–March window [30]. Corning’s own Q2 2026 SEC filing shows Optical Communications segment revenue up 32% year on year, with “Gen AI product sales growing significantly faster” than the segment average, and a new multiyear Amazon agreement covering fibre, cable and connectivity for its data-centre build-out [28]. Birla Cable’s own FY26 annual report separately states that “several producers report fully booked order books for 2026” and flags “shortages in glass preforms, helium, and polymer feedstocks” as constraining production [25].

The timing catch, in the Indian cable-makers’ own words

Birla Cable’s standalone optical-fibre-cable segment revenue fell in FY26 — ₹105.65 crore against ₹115.16 crore the year before — which the company’s own annual report attributes to “prolonged weakness…both domestically and globally,” with the year’s growth coming entirely from structured copper LAN cable instead [25][57]. The same annual report, discussing the period from April 2026, describes FY2026-27 as having “begun on a strong note…with both demand and pricing reaching record levels” [25]. Read together, these are not in conflict; they are a timeline. A version of this thesis applied to FY26 or even the first half of FY26-27 would have failed at the India cable-maker revenue line; the strong 2026 fibre story is real but recent, dated from around April 2026, not a two-year trend running back to when hyperscaler capex first accelerated.

Two different integration models

Vindhya Telelinks does not draw its own bare fibre: it sources under a long-term agreement with Birla Furukawa Fibre Optics, a joint venture, and its own filing states this leaves it “insulated to a significant extent from the bare fibre supply side risk” — a hedge against the preform bottleneck, but also a ceiling on how much of the fibre-price spike VTL itself can capture, since the JV sits in a different company (Universal Cables holds the stake, not VTL) [24][57]. Sterlite Technologies is the opposite case: management told analysts “we’re amongst the very few companies in the world to have mastered the journey from glass to gigabit…from the purest grade of silicon” — STL makes its own preform, which is a genuine differentiator against a JV-sourced peer, but it also means STL is directly exposed to whatever squeezes preform availability industry-wide, and STL has declined to disclose realised fibre or cable pricing at all: “we do not comment on a realisation” [26][53]. HFCL sits in between: it already makes some of its own preform, roughly 300 tonnes a year, and is doubling that through a new plant, but even after the expansion its own preform capacity remains a fraction of what its targeted 43–62 million fibre-km of annual fibre and cable output implies in raw-material terms [27][54].

The desk’s own finding: part of a margin recovery is tariff relief, not mix shift

The single most important qualification this note carries into the fibre leg is not from the evidence base itself but from the desk’s own Sterlite Technologies report, published after the evidence base was compiled. STL’s EBITDA margin moved from 10.3% in the December 2025 quarter to 15.1% in the March 2026 quarter to 20.8% in the June 2026 quarter — a run STL and the desk’s own earlier edition credited to AI data-centre mix shift and operating leverage. The desk’s current report finds that the same three quarters trace, almost exactly, a US tariff relief timeline: a reported ~760-basis-point tariff hit to EBITDA margin in the December 2025 quarter alone, a partial-quarter easing after a February 2026 rate reduction to 10%, and a first full quarter at the lower rate in June 2026 — the same quarter STL’s own management attributes fully to data-centre demand. STL has not disclosed how much of the recovery is which mechanism, and did not raise tariffs at all on the call covering the quarter this whole thesis rests on [53]. This does not mean the fibre story is false; STL’s own preform integration and its qualification by named-by-description hyperscalers are real. It means at least one of the cleanest-looking margin charts in this basket is not cleanly attributable to the theme it is being sold as evidence for.

A buyer says the opposite of a producer, in the same month

Black Box — a data-centre systems integrator, not a fibre producer — told its Q1 FY27 call on 13 August 2026 that “supply-chain conditions have also improved over the last quarter, particularly across cables and fiber, supporting faster project execution” [42][55]. That sits alongside fibre producers (Birla Cable, VTL, and CRU’s own data) describing continued tightness and record pricing at almost the same time. Both can be true simultaneously — a buyer’s project-execution experience in one geography is not the same measurement as a global bare-fibre and preform price index — and this is recorded as an open conflict, not resolved in either direction [29][30][42].

BEAD, tariffs and anti-dumping

The US Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment programme reached NTIA-approved Final Proposal status in all 56 states and territories only as of 25 August 2026; NTIA’s own page describes construction as “just beginning,” with other, less-verified reporting suggesting peak construction activity is still a year or more away [33]. On tariffs, HFCL is currently a net beneficiary of US trade policy toward China rather than a target of it: management told its Q1 FY27 call that a 35% US duty currently applies to Chinese fibre, while separately describing a past US duty on HFCL’s own exports that was later refunded [54]. China’s Ministry of Commerce moved the opposite way on 14 August 2026, extending anti-dumping duties of 7.4%–30.6% on Indian single-mode optical fibre for a further five years, following a review requested by China’s own domestic industry — evidence, by the fact of the protection being sought, that Indian fibre exports to China have grown large enough to warrant it [31]. The traffic has historically run the other way too: India’s own DGTR had earlier recommended anti-dumping duties on optical fibre imported from China, Korea and Indonesia, a pre-2026 action shown here only for context on how long both sides of this trade have used tariff tools against each other [32].

Optical Interconnect: The Shortage That Misses This Basket

The shortage itself is real and, on the evidence gathered, more acute than memory. TrendForce reported on 6 August 2026 that Lumentum’s CEO, Michael Hurlston, said “the supply-demand gap for indium phosphide (InP) has now surpassed that of DRAM and NAND”; customer orders have “surged to hundreds of millions of devices” from prior telecom-era quantities in the hundreds; China accounts for roughly 70% of global refined indium production; and in March 2026 NVIDIA allocated roughly $2 billion each to two key suppliers, reported elsewhere as Coherent and Lumentum, to expand capacity, with one supplier’s further expansion not targeted until end-2027 and another’s mass production not expected until 2028 [36]. Lower-confidence, secondary reporting puts the EML/pump-laser gap above 30% on Lumentum’s own May 2026 call, and estimates 800G module output running 40–60% below demand through 2027 [37][38][39]. A named Chinese InP supply chain exists — upstream metal producers, substrate and epitaxy makers, downstream module makers — and none of it overlaps with any of the seven names in this note [36].

On the evidence gathered, none of the seven has any exposure to this leg. Sterlite Technologies and Birla Cable/Vindhya Telelinks make passive glass fibre and cable, not lasers, transceivers or InP substrates. HFCL’s “Data Centre Interconnect” push, run through its HTL Limited subsidiary, is on the evidence found a fibre, cable and passive-connectivity business, not an active optical-component manufacturer — a distinction the desk’s own HFCL report makes explicitly [36][54]. Black Box and STL Networks are systems integrators that deploy other vendors’ switches and transceivers rather than making them [36][55][56]. GNG Electronics refurbishes finished devices and has no position anywhere in this supply chain.

The one Indian listed company found anywhere in this research with a genuine claim in this specific space is izmo Ltd, via its subsidiary izmo Microsystems, which unveiled silicon-photonics modules at SEMICON India on 15 September 2026 and describes itself as India’s first company doing silicon-photonics packaging — with commercial optical-transceiver launch targeted for 2027, i.e. not yet a shipping, revenue-generating product [40]. izmo is not one of the seven names in this note. If a reader’s interest in this theme is specifically the laser and transceiver shortage rather than the fibre and cable layer beneath it, nothing in this basket of seven answers that interest, and this note does not manufacture an answer where the sourcing does not support one.

Black Box: the contracted evidence

Black Box’s own Q1 FY27 call, 13 August 2026, disclosed a record order backlog of approximately US$949 million, up 83% year on year, and a new US$131 million (roughly ₹1,240 crore) three-year order from a Tier-1 US hyperscaler [42][55]. Its Capital Markets Day deck, filed 1 June 2026, separately cites a BCG-sourced bundle putting global data-centre IT-load capacity at 87 gigawatts in the recent past, growing to 233 gigawatts by 2030, and India specifically at 1,300 megawatts in 2025 rising to 7,500–8,000 megawatts by 2030 [41]. These are two different kinds of number and this note keeps them apart: the backlog and the named order are contracted; the gigawatt totals are a forecast bundle sourced to a consultancy analysis of multiple secondary inputs that cannot be individually verified figure by figure [41].

Black Box’s own record also shows the build-out’s supply chain hurting a buyer directly: at its Q3 FY26 call in February 2026, management cut full-year revenue guidance by roughly 5–7%, attributing the shortfall explicitly to “industry-wide shortages of inputs like optical fibers and cables, particularly in the booming data center ecosystem” [42][55]. Six months later, the same management described supply conditions as improved (§05). The build-out and its own physical-input shortage are, on this one company’s dated record, the same phenomenon seen from two different months.

What this does and does not mean. None of these four figures is, on its own, evidence of an accounting problem. A refurbisher buying inventory ahead of a price cycle, an infrastructure company billing government clients on long cycles, and an integrator converting a fast-growing order book into receivables before it becomes cash are all mechanically consistent with real, reported growth — working capital absorbs cash before profit becomes cash, and that is normal in businesses scaling quickly. What it does mean is that “profits are rising because of the AI data-centre theme” and “this business is generating cash from the AI data-centre theme” are two different claims, and only the first is supported by three of these four companies’ own FY26 numbers. A reader relying on reported profit alone, without checking the cash-flow statement or the receivables trend, would have missed this in every one of the four cases; each company’s own filings make it checkable, and none of the four highlights it in its own investor commentary.

Scenarios for the Theme

Three paths for the theme itself, not for any single stock, with the dated evidence that would move the reading between them. These are qualitative; no probabilities are invented.

A — Capex continues
Hyperscaler 2026 capex guidance holds or is raised further at the next round of results, memory stays tight roughly as producers describe (“well beyond calendar 2027” per Micron), and India’s announced megawatt pipeline starts converting from announced to under-construction at a visible pace. What would show it: Meta, Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon each reiterating or raising 2026-27 capex at their next reported quarters; TrendForce’s Q4 2026 contract call (expected around the turn of the quarter) landing at or above the current 3–8% PC DRAM range rather than below it; a named India data-centre project moving from “announced” to a disclosed construction start. Under this path, the two names with company-disclosed, growing data-centre revenue shares — Sterlite Technologies and HFCL — have the most direct, checkable line to it; Black Box’s backlog would be expected to keep compounding; the rest of the basket would benefit only at the distance already described in §09.
B — Memory decelerates as forecast (the base case already in motion)
TrendForce’s own chained forecasts play out largely as published: PC DRAM contract increases fall from over 100% in Q1 2026 toward 3–8% in Q4 2026, the rate of increase keeps slowing while the price level itself does not reverse, and 2027 remains directionally tight but unquantified by any producer. What would show it: TrendForce’s actual Q4 2026 call against its own 3–8% forecast; Micron’s fiscal Q4 2026 results, due 30 September 2026, eleven days after the evidence base was compiled and the first Micron print not yet reflected in this note; each company’s Q2 FY27 results in October–November 2026, particularly GNG’s gross margin against its record 24.65% and whether cost of goods per device starts overtaking revenue per device. This is the path every primary source reviewed for §03 currently points toward, and it is compatible with the fibre and build-out legs continuing to hold at the same time — deceleration in memory is not the same claim as a pause in data-centre capex.
C — Capex pauses
One or more hyperscalers cut guidance rather than raise it, echoing the mechanism Black Box itself already lived through once, when a data-centre input shortage (not a demand pause) forced a guidance cut in February 2026. A genuine pause would show up first as a change in hyperscaler capex language at results, then as allocation frameworks like Sterlite’s two hyperscaler deals converting to purchase orders more slowly than their headline values imply, then as India’s already-thin operational megawatt growth slowing further. What would show it: any of Meta, Microsoft, Alphabet or Amazon guiding 2027 capex down rather than up; STL’s order book failing to move at its Q2 FY27 print despite the two new allocation deals; a resolution of the proposed additional India tariff CRISIL flags in a direction that reopens the margin pressure STL saw in the December 2025 quarter; BEAD funding decisions or disbursement that slip further given construction NTIA itself still describes as “just beginning.” This is the path least supported by anything gathered for this note as of 20 September 2026, but it is the path every capacity-heavy name in this basket — Sterlite, HFCL, and the merged Birla Cable/Vindhya Telelinks entity once it completes — is most exposed to, precisely because each is adding capacity into demand that is still substantially a forecast rather than a backlog.

Key Risks

01
Trading the interconnect narrative through names with zero interconnect exposure
The global laser and indium-phosphide shortage is the most dramatic shortage language in the whole theme, and none of the seven names in this basket makes transceivers, lasers or InP substrates. A position taken on this basket because of interconnect headlines is a position on a leg that provably does not apply to it.
02
The two names with the clearest measured exposure carry the exchange’s most severe surveillance stage
Sterlite Technologies and HFCL both show a company-disclosed, growing data-centre revenue share, and both are under long-term ASM surveillance at its most severe stage as of the 18 September scan. Surveillance-listed scrips typically carry raised margin requirements and routine exclusion from broker margin-funding lists; confirm eligibility with your broker before assuming either can be held on margin [53][54].
03
Cash conversion lagged or went negative at three names in the very year profits rose
GNG Electronics and HFCL both reported negative operating cash flow in FY26 against record or near-record reported profit; Black Box converted only about 39% of profit into operating cash the same year. None of this is disqualifying on its own, but it means the profit trend and the cash trend are telling different stories at three of the seven (§10).
04
Memory’s rate of increase is already decelerating, on the theme’s own primary source
TrendForce’s own chained forecasts move from over 100% quarterly PC DRAM contract growth in Q1 2026 to 3–8% by Q4 2026. This is the one leg of the theme with a quantified forecast, and the forecast itself already points toward deceleration in the rate, if not yet the level, of the very price rise the basket is being traded on.
05
The fibre “super-cycle” is young, and priced in places as if it were mature
Birla Cable’s own FY26 optical-fibre-cable revenue fell, with the company’s own text dating the strong pricing environment to April 2026 onward. Tapes across this basket have moved on a timeline longer than the fibre story itself has been confirmed to hold.
06
Two of Sterlite’s three hyperscaler-linked disclosures are allocation frameworks, not backlog
The US$1.11bn and US$288m contracts both describe a ceiling on future purchase orders released periodically, not a fixed delivery schedule. Either can under-deliver against its headline value for reasons that have nothing to do with the data-centre demand thesis being wrong, and STL’s order book has not been revised to reflect either deal as of the last confirmed disclosure [53].
07
STL Networks is priced adjacent to a theme it has zero measured exposure to
No named hyperscaler, cloud-provider or private AI contract exists anywhere in STL Networks’ disclosed order book; every named, dated contract is government or PSU. The desk’s own report on this name carries an Avoid rating for reasons independent of, but consistent with, this note’s finding [56].
08
Birla Cable trades well above its own swap-implied value into an unfinished merger
At the 18 September 2026 close, Birla Cable traded 77.0% above the value its own agreed swap ratio would pay in Vindhya Telelinks shares, up from 39.6% on the last close before the boards approved the scheme. Both companies’ public shareholders must still vote and the scheme must clear the NCLT before completion [57].
09
Everyone is adding fibre and cable capacity into demand that is itself still partly a forecast
HFCL, Birla Cable/Vindhya Telelinks, Corning with NVIDIA, Prysmian and Fujikura are all separately expanding capacity into the same demand pool. Optical fibre has a documented history of new capacity arriving quickly once margins turn attractive, which is the industry’s own current, self-reported condition [25][27][28][34].
10
Policy and litigation risk sits alongside the commercial risk
CRISIL’s own June 2026 rationale on Sterlite names a proposed additional 12.5% India tariff as a live, unresolved monitorable, on top of a $96.5 million US trade-secret verdict against an STL subsidiary that remains under appeal. China’s extension of anti-dumping duties on Indian fibre, and the still-forming EU Critical Medicines-style policy apparatus around critical materials generally, are both reminders that a global, government-adjacent supply chain carries government-adjacent risk [31][53].

Summary

VERDICT: THEMATIC SYNTHESIS — THE COMMODITY STORY HOLDS FAR MORE CONSISTENTLY THAN THE EQUITY-EXPOSURE STORY DOES; NOT A RECOMMENDATION

The theme the user asked about is real where it is furthest from these seven companies, and uneven where it is closest to them. Memory pricing holds strongly on primary producer evidence — Samsung, SK hynix, Micron and Kioxia each independently reporting 30–85% quarterly selling-price increases — but its clearest reach into this basket is a cost line at one refurbisher, not a revenue line at six manufacturers. Chip and packaging pricing holds only for advanced, AI-specific packaging, and none of the seven touches it directly. The data-centre build-out holds as a real and growing pool of hyperscaler capital, but India’s own operational capacity is a fraction of the announced totals, and the gap between announced, contracted and built is exactly where a reader should be most careful.

Optical fibre and cable is the leg closest to what four of these seven actually sell, and it is also the one carrying the sharpest timing catch. Birla Cable’s own optical-fibre revenue fell in FY26; the strong pricing environment its own annual report describes dates from April 2026, not from whenever the AI capex story began. Sterlite Technologies’ own margin recovery, credited to data-centre mix shift, tracks a US tariff-relief timeline closely enough that this note cannot separate the two mechanisms from anything Sterlite itself has disclosed. HFCL’s own data-centre-labelled revenue is real and company-disclosed, and it is also a small slice — roughly one-twentieth of Q1 FY27 revenue — of a much larger story. Optical interconnect, the leg with the most dramatic shortage language of all, fails cleanly: none of the seven makes a transceiver, a laser, or an InP substrate, and nothing in this research changes that.

The exposure map in §09 is the note’s central finding. Two names, Sterlite Technologies and HFCL, carry a genuinely measured and growing company-disclosed data-centre share. Black Box has real contracted hyperscale business but no clean disclosed share of it. GNG Electronics is exposed only through a cost line. STL Networks, Birla Cable and Vindhya Telelinks carry no company-disclosed data-centre-specific figure at all, and STL Networks in particular has no named AI or hyperscale contract of any kind despite sharing a demerger history with a name that does. Underneath the profit lines, GNG, HFCL and Black Box each show operating cash flow lagging or negative in the very year reported profit rose, and STL Networks shows the same mechanism through receivables stretching toward a year of sales — a pattern that says growth is consuming working capital, not that anything is wrong, but a pattern none of the four volunteers in its own investor commentary.

This note changes no rating the desk has already published. It carries no position-sizing, allocation or entry/exit view on any of the seven, and it is not a recommendation.
