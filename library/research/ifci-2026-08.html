02 —

Read This First: The Lending Business Is Not the Investment

IFCI is the most unusual name in this entire library, and applying normal financial analysis to it will produce a badly wrong answer.

Look at the operating metrics honestly:

MetricValueWhat it would normally mean
Gross NPA ratio95.68%Almost the entire loan book is non-performing
CRAR−17.58%Negative capital adequacy, against an RBI minimum of 15%
Fresh lendingstopped ~FY22No new business being written
Q1 FY27 revenue from operations₹327.06 Crdown from ₹407.18 Cr

By any conventional measure this is a failed lender. A negative capital adequacy ratio means the institution has negative regulatory capital — for a private NBFC that would be terminal.

It is not terminal here because IFCI is government-owned and is being restructured rather than resolved. The market is not pricing the lending book at all. It is pricing a look-through claim on the National Stock Exchange, and everything in Sections 05 to 08 follows from that.

Note the gap between consolidated (₹60.27 Cr) and standalone (₹8.64 Cr) profit. Almost all of IFCI’s earnings come from subsidiaries — principally SHCIL — rather than from the parent. That is the numerical confirmation of Section 02: the holding structure is the business.

Two metrics moved in the right direction: gross NPA edged down from 95.79% to 95.68%, and CRAR improved from −18.78% to −17.58%. Both are improvements from catastrophic to slightly less catastrophic, and they matter mainly as evidence that the restructuring is progressing.

05 —

The NSE Chain

LinkStake
IFCI → SHCIL52.86%
SHCIL → National Stock Exchange~4.44%
IFCI’s effective look-through NSE stake~2.3%

The NSE is India’s dominant equity exchange and is unlisted, with a long-anticipated IPO. Exchanges are among the best businesses in finance — near-monopoly network effects, high margins, capital-light. A listed vehicle offering exposure to a pre-IPO NSE stake is a genuinely scarce asset, and that scarcity is what the price reflects.

Three cautions on the arithmetic:

  • 2.3% is a look-through figure, twice removed. IFCI does not own NSE shares; it owns just over half of a company that owns 4.44% of them.
  • The NSE IPO has been anticipated for years. Regulatory approval, timing and eventual valuation are all outside IFCI’s control.
  • A holding-company discount normally applies to exactly this structure — which is what makes Section 06 the most important development in the story.
06 —

The SHCIL Merger — The Real News Since June

The Finance Ministry has approved the merger of SHCIL into IFCI.

This is the single most consequential change since the June report, and the mechanism is worth stating precisely: a merger removes the holding-company discount, because the assets come directly onto IFCI’s balance sheet rather than sitting one layer down in a 52.86%-owned subsidiary.

Three effects follow:

  • The look-through NSE stake becomes a direct stake. Post-merger, IFCI would hold the NSE shares itself rather than through SHCIL, converting an indirect ~2.3% into a directly held position.
  • It brings SHCIL’s capital onto IFCI’s balance sheet, which is directly relevant to the −17.58% CRAR problem. Merging a well-capitalised subsidiary into an under-capitalised parent is a route to repairing capital adequacy without fresh cash.
  • It simplifies a structure that has been the main analytical obstacle to valuing the company.

Ministry approval is not completion. Scheme approval, creditor and shareholder consents, NCLT sanction and a record date all lie between approval and effect, and the timetable is not established in the sources reviewed. Treat this as a sanctioned intention with real execution ahead of it.

07 —

Government Support

Equity infusion approved₹500 Cr, January 2026
Approving authorityGovernment of India

A ₹500 crore infusion against ₹1,786 crore of net worth is a meaningful addition — roughly 28% — and it is the practical reason a lender with negative capital adequacy continues as a going concern.

This is the fact that underwrites the entire investment case and it deserves to be named plainly: IFCI is solvent because the Government of India chooses to keep it solvent. That is a policy dependency, not a commercial one. It has been reliable, and the merger approval suggests continued intent — but it is discretionary, and a change in policy priorities is a genuine, if low-probability, risk that has no analogue in any other name in this library.

What You Are Actually Buying

Stripping the analysis to its structure, an IFCI holder at ₹24,276 crore is buying:

ComponentCharacter
A ~2.3% look-through stake in the unlisted NSEThe asset — scarce, high quality, no public mark
Control of SHCIL (custody and depository services)A real operating business, source of most consolidated profit
A loan book with 95.68% gross NPAs in run-offA liability wearing an asset’s clothing
Negative regulatory capital (−17.58% CRAR)Repaired by government support and, prospectively, the merger
Government ownership and policy supportThe thing that holds the structure together

This is a structure trade, not an operating-company investment. Nothing in the quarterly P&L will tell you whether it works. The variables that matter are the merger completing, the NSE IPO happening, and the valuation the NSE eventually achieves — and none of the three is under IFCI’s control or visible in its results.

That is an unusual thing to own, and it should be sized as such rather than as a financial-sector position.

The last line divides the ₹24,276 Cr market capitalisation by the ~2.3% effective NSE stake. It attributes the entire market value to the NSE holding and gives zero credit to SHCIL’s operating business or the residual loan book, so it overstates the implied NSE valuation. It is shown as a framing device, not a valuation.

The honest position is that IFCI cannot be valued from its financial statements. A P/E of 135× on a company whose earnings are incidental tells you nothing. The only way to form a view is to take a position on what the NSE is worth, apply the ~2.3% look-through, discount for the structure and the wait, and compare that to ₹24,276 crore — and the first term in that chain is a private valuation nobody can observe.

That is not a reason to dismiss it. It is a reason to recognise that this is a bet on a private-market valuation, dressed in a listed wrapper, and to hold it with the uncertainty that implies.

Scenarios

Bull
The SHCIL merger completes, collapsing the holding-company discount and bringing the NSE stake directly onto IFCI’s balance sheet while repairing capital adequacy. The NSE IPO proceeds and prices at a strong valuation, giving the ~2.3% stake a public mark for the first time. A structural discount becomes a visible asset value, and the re-rating is mechanical.
Base
The merger proceeds through its regulatory steps over several quarters. The NSE IPO stays anticipated rather than executed, as it has for years. The stock continues trading on news flow around both events, with no earnings anchor to hold it in either direction. Government support continues and CRAR keeps improving slowly.
Bear
The NSE IPO is delayed again or prices below expectations. The merger encounters regulatory or NCLT delay. Attention returns to the underlying entity: 95.68% gross NPAs, negative capital adequacy, no fresh lending, and a market capitalisation many times stated net worth. There is no earnings floor and no asset mark to defend the price.

13 —

Summary

RATING: HOLD — A STRUCTURE TRADE, NOT A LENDER

IFCI is the one name in this library where conventional analysis actively misleads. Gross NPAs of 95.68% and a capital adequacy ratio of −17.58% would be terminal for any private lender. It survives because it is government-owned and being restructured — a ₹500 crore infusion in January and, now, Finance Ministry approval to merge SHCIL into IFCI.

That merger is the real development since the June report, and it is a genuine positive: it collapses the holding-company discount, brings the NSE stake directly onto the balance sheet, and helps repair capital adequacy without fresh cash. Ministry approval is not completion, though, and the steps between are not on a stated timetable.

What you are buying at ₹24,276 crore is a ~2.3% look-through claim on the unlisted NSE, plus control of SHCIL, minus a loan book that is 96% non-performing. Nothing in the quarterly results will tell you whether that works — the variables are the merger completing, the NSE listing, and the valuation it achieves, none of which IFCI controls. Your own signals have it passing the trend template on RS 83, but what that momentum is tracking is a policy narrative rather than a business.
