02 —

The Business

A heat exchanger coil is the component that actually moves heat inside an air conditioner or refrigeration unit. KRN makes these for HVAC original equipment manufacturers — it is a tier-one component supplier inside someone else’s appliance, in the same structural position as TD Power Systems is to turbine makers.

Why the demand is strong

Three things are compounding at once. Indian air-conditioning penetration remains low and is rising quickly with incomes. Global HVAC OEMs are actively diversifying component sourcing away from single-country dependence, which favours qualified Indian suppliers. And commercial refrigeration is expanding with organised retail and cold-chain build-out.

Why the position is fragile

Coils are a manufactured commodity with engineering content, not a proprietary product. The customer is an OEM with in-house engineering capability who can qualify a second source, and in some cases can make coils itself. KRN’s protection is cost, quality consistency and capacity availability — real advantages, but not the kind that support a triple-digit earnings multiple on their own.

04 —

Exports

Exports reached ₹52.38 crore, up 177.31%, lifting the export share from 16.38% to 20.76% of segment sales.

This is the most strategically encouraging line in the results. Winning export share in HVAC components means passing the qualification processes of global OEMs — a slow, demanding process that, once cleared, is durable. It also directly validates the “China-plus-one component sourcing” thesis rather than merely asserting it.

At 20.76% the export book is balanced rather than concentrated — a healthier profile than TD Power Systems, where 93% of new order inflow is export-driven. KRN retains a large domestic base while building an export franchise, which is the more resilient of the two structures.

05 —

The ₹350 Crore QIP

KRN completed a ₹350 crore qualified institutional placement during the period.

Read this carefully, because it cuts in two directions and both matter:

  • The positive. Institutional investors committed ₹350 crore of fresh capital at prevailing prices. That is external validation and it funds capacity expansion without adding debt, which is the right way to finance growth in a capital-intensive component business.
  • The dilution. ₹350 crore against a ₹10,345 crore market capitalisation is roughly 3.4% of the company issued to new holders. Existing shareholders own proportionally less of every future rupee of profit, and the per-share earnings arithmetic resets accordingly.

There is also a signalling point worth naming plainly: companies raise equity when management believes the stock is fairly valued or better. A QIP at 107× trailing earnings is a rational corporate decision, and it is information about how management views the price.

06 —

The Rajasthan Incentive

The company has received a sanctioned incentive of ₹182.95 crore from the state of Rajasthan.

Against annualised profit of roughly ₹130 crore (Q1 PAT of ₹32.9 crore × 4), an incentive of ₹182.95 crore is a large number — larger than a full year of current earnings. State industrial incentives of this kind typically accrue over several years and are often linked to investment and employment milestones rather than being received upfront.

The accounting treatment matters more than the headline. Whether this is recognised as revenue, as other income, as a capital reserve, or netted against asset cost changes how it flows through reported earnings — and therefore how much of any future “profit growth” is operating performance versus subsidy recognition. This is the specific line to check in the annual report before extrapolating margins.

08 —

Customer Position

As an OEM component supplier, KRN’s revenue is concentrated among a limited number of HVAC manufacturers. This is inherent to the model and is not a criticism — it is how tier-one supply works — but it carries specific consequences:

  • Order visibility is short. OEM component supply runs on schedules and releases, not multi-year backlogs. There is no order book here to lean on the way HFCL or Jyoti CNC holders can.
  • Growth is customer-decision-dependent. A single OEM adding or shifting a programme moves the revenue line materially in either direction.
  • Backward integration is a live risk. HVAC OEMs with scale can and sometimes do bring coil manufacturing in-house.

The mitigating factor is that KRN has been winning export qualification, which broadens the customer base beyond domestic OEMs and reduces single-customer dependence over time.

Scenarios

Bull
The ₹350 Cr QIP funds capacity that fills immediately. Indian AC penetration and global component-sourcing diversification keep demand ahead of capacity for two to three years. Export share climbs past 30%, broadening the customer base and reducing OEM concentration. Earnings grow into the multiple rather than the multiple falling to the earnings.
Base
Growth moderates from 119% toward a still-strong 30–50% as the base rises. Margins hold near current levels. The business is genuinely good, and the stock goes sideways for an extended period while earnings catch up to a 107× starting multiple. Time, not price, does the correcting.
Bear
HVAC demand normalises after a strong cycle, or an OEM in-sources coil production, or competitors add capacity into the same opportunity. Growth decelerates sharply. At 106.7× earnings and 17.15× book with an ST-ASM flag and no order book to cushion, the de-rating would be severe. The 200-DMA at ₹1,086 is 31% below spot.

Summary

RATING: HOLD — EXCELLENT GROWTH, EXTRAORDINARY MULTIPLE

KRN produced the strongest operating quarter in your portfolio: revenue up 119%, profit up 164%, net margin up 227 basis points, exports up 177% and now a fifth of segment sales. It raised ₹350 crore from institutions to fund capacity and secured a large state incentive. Every operating fact is positive, and the stock is your best percentage performer.

It is also priced at 106.7× trailing earnings and 17.15× book — the most expensive name you own on earnings — in a business that is a component supplier to OEMs, with no order book, concentrated customers, no proprietary product, and a surveillance flag.

Those two paragraphs are both accurate, and the tension between them is the analysis. This is not a company with a problem; it is a company whose share price has already extrapolated several years of the current trajectory. The relevant question is not “is KRN a good business” — it plainly is — but “what happens to a 107× multiple when growth decelerates from 119% to a merely excellent 40%”.
