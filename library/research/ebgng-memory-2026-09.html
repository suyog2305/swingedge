The framework and the fundamentals disagree again, and this time the disagreement can be measured. Every signal the desk runs is favourable, and the last reported quarter carried the best gross margin the company has printed. Against that stand three things that came out of the documents rather than out of the price. First, FY26 operating cash flow was −₹215.3 Cr against a reported profit of ₹132.0 Cr [6] — the number two earlier editions asked for and did not have. Second, the forecaster whose figures built this thesis now forecasts the quarterly rise in PC DRAM contract prices fading from more than 100% in the first quarter of 2026 to 3–8% in the fourth [8][13]. The short answer to the question this edition was asked: memory reaches GNG through seven channels. Three help and are in the reported figures already. Two hurt with a lag and mostly are not in them yet. One is not a business here, and one is a promise rather than a number. And the market stopped being late to this story in mid-June.

The spot market is small and volatile and is where brokers and module makers trade day to day; most memory is sold to PC makers, server makers and phone makers on quarterly contracts, and it is contract prices that set the cost of a new laptop. The producers’ own average-selling-price changes further down are reported facts, but they blend price with product mix. Two cautions keep that honest. The year-on-year widening of gross margin did not start with memory: Q2 FY26 (July–September 2025, before management’s own 1 October base date) was already 540 basis points above its prior year, at 19.9% against 14.5%, which management then put down to buying better, selling better and the move to its own brand [5]. And the sequential jump from Q4 to Q1 flatters, because Q4 is the quarter in which, in the director’s words, there is “always a push of volume over margins” [3]. What is specific to this year is the level: 24.65% is the highest gross margin the company has reported, against a 19.2–21.4% band for the whole of FY26.

(a) The selling price — two umbrellas, not one

There are two comparisons here and they get run together. The first is the peg: a refurbished business laptop sells at about 30% of the price of an equivalent new business laptop — the prospectus shows a refurbished Dell Latitude 7490 at ₹23,250 against ₹85,000 new [7] — and the managing director says trust could take that toward the 50% that used cars and phones command, adding that even a move from 30% to 35% would be a large margin gain [1]. The second is substitution: a buyer who wanted a new entry-level laptop finds it now costs ₹48,000 and that a high-end refurbished one costs about ₹30,800 [1]. Management put the crossover plainly in May: the end user can now get “a premium high-end refurbished computer at a better value” than an entry-level new one [3].

Both umbrellas are a long way above GNG’s price, and that is the under-appreciated half of the deceleration argument. GNG has taken 12% while the entry-level new machine, on its own figures, went up 88%. If new-laptop prices merely stop rising, nothing forces GNG’s realisation down; the gap is wide enough that it would take actual cuts to new-PC prices to press on it. TrendForce, for what it is worth, was still expecting retail notebook prices to “rise across the board” in the third quarter as dearer components worked through PC makers’ inventories [11] — new-PC prices lag contract prices, just as used-PC prices lag new ones.

The volume half is less convincing than the price half. Unit growth, derived from the figures management has given, ran at roughly +17% in the first half of FY26, +30% in Q3 (186,000 against 143,000), about +27% in Q4 (241,000 against roughly 189,000), and back to about +18% in Q1 FY27 [4][3][1]. The quarter with the widest price umbrella had the slowest unit growth of the last three. An analyst put the slowdown to management directly; the answer was that Q1 is “a low seasonality quarter” — which does not explain a year-on-year rate — and “I don’t think growth will slow down” [1]. The reading most consistent with the rest of the record is that GNG chose price over volume (“we are not going to compromise on margins” [4]), which is what the September edition’s European data also showed for the industry [32]. Substitution is showing up as realisation and mix, not as a flood of units.

What can be said is how large the exposure is per unit touched. At the closing FY26 exchange rate implied by the annual report, about ₹94.3 to the dollar [6], management’s own 30 June price of $126 for an 8GB DDR5 module is about ₹11,900, against about ₹2,200 for the $23.35 it quoted for 1 October 2025; its ~$249 for a 1TB SSD is about ₹23,500 against about ₹6,600 [1][3].

Three inferences follow, and they are labelled as inferences. First, at these prices GNG is unlikely to be fitting new memory to any large share of units — the arithmetic would show in the margin, and the margin went up. “Repair rather than replace” is doing real work. Second, and more important, GNG is structurally long memory, not short it: every used laptop it buys arrives with RAM and, usually, a drive already inside, acquired at a used-machine price that management says trails the new one [1]. The loose modules it buys for upgrades and repairs are the smaller, short leg of that position. Third, the short leg is probably dearer than management’s DDR5 quotes suggest, because the fleet is DDR4-generation and DDR4 is the part of the market being wound down [25][17]. None of the three is quantified by any source, and the AGM on 24 September is the nearest place to ask.

One industry practice would matter a great deal and could not be sourced to any GNG document, so it is posed as a question and not used: enterprises and their disposal contractors often remove or destroy drives before releasing machines, which would make SSDs — on management’s figures up about three and a half times — the component a refurbisher buys most often.

(d) Procurement — the lag is the trade

Everything GNG has earned from memory so far rests on one sentence from the 30 July call: used-machine prices “are trailing. They are not happening in tandem” [1]. New-laptop prices rose within weeks of component prices; what a bank or a leasing company accepts for a three-year-old fleet has moved much more slowly. GNG sells against the first and buys against the second. Management says it has so far secured material “almost at the similar prices” and “at the old prices because of our contracts”, and expects “more goodness… in the coming quarters” [1].

The same record says the lag closes. In February: “the current used inventory is coming at a higher price” [4]. In May: “our input cost will also go up” [3]. In July, on whether dearer new machines mean fewer disposals: companies may defer purchases or extend leases, and the pool of disposals gets smaller — “absolutely, yes” [1]. The prospectus had it as a risk factor before any of this began: “a slowdown in corporate IT upgrades could reduce the volume of used laptops available” [7]. Gartner expects business device lifetimes to lengthen 15% and PC shipments to fall 10.4% in 2026 [20]; IDC, as management quotes it, has shipments down 11.3% for the year and about 20% in the second half [1].

Management’s answer is the inventory itself — buy more, from more sellers, earlier. The supplier count has gone from 557 to 601 to more than 720 to 773 across four disclosures [5][6][1], and the deals it describes are large and lumpy: about 60,000 higher-generation desktops from one US bank, a bank in Colombia, a customer in Australia taking 3,000 laptops in one order “because only we had the inventory” [1]. That is a coherent strategy. It also means channel (d) cannot be read separately from channel (c): the cost of defending supply is the working capital in §06. The one number that tracks it is cost of goods per device, up about 7% in a year (derived, §04). Fewer machines sold into the channel in 2026–27 is a problem for 2028–29 volumes, when those machines would have come up for refurbishment; nothing reported so far can show it.

(e) Component harvesting and parts resale — not a business here

A refurbisher sitting on hundreds of thousands of machines full of memory that has quintupled could, in principle, strip and sell it. There is no evidence GNG does. The revenue note has three lines — sales in India, other operational income of ₹26.5 Cr, and sales outside India — and none is parts [6]. The prospectus describes in-house repair of parts and reclaiming of plastics to raise yield, which is harvesting for its own use [7]. Asked about IT-asset disposal in February, the managing director said the company wants “to remain on the refurbishment piece” [4]. The sign is zero until a disclosure says otherwise, and this report does not pencil in a benefit.

(f) Servers — the channel where memory matters most, and the one with no numbers

Memory is a far larger share of a server’s value than of a laptop’s, and server DRAM is where TrendForce sees the shortage lasting longest — forecast at about +90% in Q1 2026 and +13–18% in Q3, with a shortage already anticipated for 2027 [8][12]. GNG announced its “entry into infrastructure level refurbishment” on 4 November 2025, saying it had taken space in Mumbai, the UAE and the US for “servers, data centers, hardware, and enterprise-grade IT infrastructure” [5]. The annual report lists servers in the portfolio [6].

That is all there is. No server revenue, unit count or margin has been disclosed. Laptops were 81% of revenue in Q1 FY27 and everything else — desktops, tablets, servers, phones, workstations, accessories — 19% [1]. The word “server” does not appear in the transcripts of 5 February, 5 May or 30 July 2026 [4][3][1]. A bull is entitled to call it an option; this desk will not put a value on a line the company has not reported since announcing it ten months ago.

How much of Q1 FY27 was the inventory?

The company has not quantified it and this report will not pretend to. An upper bound is available. That cuts both ways, and §09 uses it.

Half two — since mid-June the price has run ahead of the print, twice

Between 12 June and 2 July 2026 the shares rose 56% in three weeks (derived), with no GNG number of any kind. Read against this table, the August edition’s puzzle has a plain answer: the June buyers had already paid for the July print, and they had done it the day before TrendForce said the rise was moderating.

What Would Falsify the Thesis — and the Deceleration, Dealt With

TrendForce’s own forecasts have the quarterly rise in PC DRAM contract prices going from more than 100% to 58–63% to 15–20% to 3–8% across 2026 [8][9][13] — the second figure being its call for conventional DRAM as a whole, since the record holds no separate PC figure for that quarter, and the last two reaching the desk at second hand. A thesis built on rising memory has to say what that does to it. The answer depends on which of the seven channels is doing the work, because they respond to different things.

The rise fades roughly as TrendForce forecasts and the level holds. November’s print is good — stock bought cheaply in the spring is still being sold — and settles nothing. Through the second half of FY27 cost per device catches up with revenue per device and gross margin drifts from 24.65% back toward 21–22%: better than FY26, short of Q1. FY27 delivers the guided 30% and the margin gain, which is about ₹190–197 Cr of profit on this report’s arithmetic. Working capital stays heavy and cash conversion improves only modestly. A good company compounds at 25–30% while a 56× multiple compresses toward that growth rate, in the abrupt steps this stock moves in.
Bear
A contract call with a minus sign arrives in 2027, or PC makers cut prices to defend volumes, and the umbrella itself comes down while ₹700 Cr of stock — some of it bought at 2026 prices — is still on the shelf. Meanwhile the lagged negatives land together: enterprises that kept their fleets an extra year release fewer machines, sellers have re-priced what they do release, and DDR4 parts stay dear because the standard is being discontinued. Gross margin goes back through 20%; finance cost, a quarter of EBITDA today, does not. Receivables stay at 40–45 days by design. On thin coverage, a narrow float, CARE BBB-rated working-capital lines [32] and a multiple above 50, the de-rating is fast — July showed a 22% fall in a month on good news.

Key Risks

01
The price has run ahead of the print twice, and the second time is now
+56% in three weeks of June before the Q1 FY27 results, then limit-down on a record quarter; +33% since that results-day close on no new reported number [31]. About four-fifths of the advance since May is multiple, not earnings (derived). At ~56× trailing and ~41–42× the profit guidance implies, the November print has to clear a bar the July print, which was excellent, did not.
02
Profit does not convert to cash
Net cash used in operations was ₹215.3 Cr in FY26 and ₹113.8 Cr in FY25, against profits of ₹132.0 Cr and ₹69.0 Cr [6]. Inventory, receivables at 40–45 days by policy, and payables of about six days mean the company funds the entire cycle. The IPO’s ₹400 Cr paid for FY26. Management foresees no equity raise through 2028–29 and is comfortable to about ₹450 Cr of net debt [3].
03
Part of the record margin is a holding gain that needs rising prices
Management confirms an inventory gain (“Absolutely”) [1]. If Q1 FY27 had earned last year’s 21.4% gross margin, pre-tax profit would have been about ₹13.6 Cr, or 38%, lower (derived, an upper bound) [2]. TrendForce forecasts the quarterly rise in PC DRAM fading to 3–8% by Q4 2026 [13]; carrying the stock costs about 2.3–2.5% a quarter (derived).
04
Used-machine supply shrinks and re-prices, with a lag
Management: used prices “are trailing”, and the pool of disposals will be lower, “absolutely, yes” [1]. The prospectus flagged it [7]; Gartner expects business device lives to lengthen 15% [20]. The defence is more inventory from more suppliers, which is risk 02 again. Cost of goods per device is already up about 7% in a year (derived).
05
Memory is also an input, and the exposure is undisclosed
The prospectus lists SSDs and RAM among major purchased materials [7]; no document gives their share of cost, and inventory is reported as a single line. One 8GB DDR5 module at June prices is about ₹11,900 against gross profit per device of ~₹6,780 (derived). The fleet is DDR4-generation, and DDR4 is dearer than DDR5 at spot as it is discontinued [17][25].
06
The deceleration rows rest on the weakest sourcing in the record
The Q3 and Q4 2026 PC DRAM calls reached the desk third-hand [13]; three of five producer price figures did not come from the producer’s own document [19][21][23][24]; the desk has one day of spot history [17]; no one has published a quantified 2027 forecast. The direction is well attested. The decimals are not.
07
Disclosure on the central driver lives in speech, and speech has been loose
The Q1 FY27 presentation does not contain the words memory, RAM, SSD, component or inventory — confirmed again for this report by searching its text [2]. On the calls, memory inflation was “more than doubled” then corrected to five times [1]; input cost was “reasonably insulated” then “will also go up” [3]; March net debt was ₹300-odd Cr in May and ₹399 Cr in July [3][1]; relief was “end of 2027” in February and “January 2028” in May [4][3].
08
Unhedged currency and a single overseas hub
No hedging policy at the IPO [7]; 64% of revenue and 69% of short-term borrowings are outside India [1][6]. FY26 carried a ₹29.5 Cr translation gain and ₹36.1 Cr of translation in inventory that a stronger rupee would reverse [6]. All overseas processing runs through Sharjah, by air, in a region that, on management’s own account, came under missile and drone attack during 2026 [3][1]; about two-thirds of FY25 revenue was routed through the UAE subsidiary [29].
09
Servers are a story, not a segment
Announced 4 November 2025 [5]; no revenue, units or margin since; absent from the three calls of 2026 [4][3][1]. Any valuation that credits it is crediting an intention.
10
Narrow float, thin coverage, and surveillance status
Promoters held 74.77% at June 2026 and sold about ₹175 Cr at ₹390 on 11 June [32]; two brokers cover the stock and the price is above one target and 3% below the other [29][32]. Weekly correlation with the memory makers is near zero (0.11 with Micron), so the stock moves on its own dates and in large steps [31]. No ASM, ESM or GSM flag applies on the 18 September list [30]; re-check at every scan, because a flag would raise margins and usually removes a stock from broker margin-funding lists.

Summary

RATING: HOLD AT CMP — MEMORY WIDENS THE SPREAD, BUT THE PRICE GOT THERE FIRST

The rating is unchanged from 8 September. The reasoning is more specific, and one part of it is new. The question put to this edition was how, exactly, memory prices reach GNG’s profit and loss, in which direction, and how much of it is already in the numbers and the price.

How. Through seven channels. Dearer memory makes new laptops dearer, which lifts what GNG can charge and pushes buyers toward it (a): laptop realisation is up 12% in a year and units about 18%. It lifts the value of stock bought before the rise (c), which management confirms. It helps most where GNG earns most, in the US and Europe at a gross margin near 30%, and a weaker rupee has added to it (g). Those are the positives and all three are in the reported numbers: gross profit per device held at ₹5,000–5,450 for four quarters and then stepped to about ₹6,780 in April–June 2026, as each device sold for about 12% more and cost about 7% more (derived). Against them, GNG buys RAM and SSDs itself (b) — its prospectus says so, and no document says how much — and the used machines it depends on get scarcer and dearer as enterprises keep fleets longer (d), which the managing director conceded in July in the words “absolutely, yes”. Those two arrive with a lag and are barely in the numbers yet. Parts resale (e) is not a business here. Servers (f), where memory matters most, are an announcement from November 2025 with no number behind it.

What it cost. The trade was placed in inventory, and the cash flow statement earlier editions lacked shows the bill: operating cash flow of −₹215.3 Cr in FY26 on ₹132.0 Cr of profit, funded by the IPO. Stock stands at about ₹700 Cr, around six months of cost, financed at 9–10%. If Q1 FY27 had earned last year’s gross margin its pre-tax profit would have been up to 38% lower.

Whether the market had priced it. For four months it had not — GNG made its post-listing low on 27 January 2026 while the memory makers had more than doubled — and it re-rated only with the company’s own first numbers in February. Since mid-June it has twice run ahead of the print: +56% in three weeks before the July results, which were excellent and were met with a lower circuit, and +33% since, to a record close, on nothing reported. About four-fifths of the advance since May is multiple. The lateness that made this stock interesting has been spent.

The deceleration. TrendForce forecasts the quarterly rise in PC DRAM contracts fading from over 100% to 3–8% during 2026. That does not remove the umbrella, which depends on the level of new-PC prices; it removes the holding gain, which depends on the rate, while the lagged costs keep arriving. Because GNG turns its stock in about two quarters, November’s margin should still look strong whichever case is right. The test that matters is cost per device against revenue per device, quarter by quarter, into early 2027.

Hold at CMP is a statement about the entry and the evidence, not about the company. The business is better than the August edition thought and its tailwind is better documented than most. The framework in §08 has no objection. But a record multiple is being paid for a record margin that is partly a one-off, funded by cash the business does not yet generate, ahead of two prints that cannot settle the question — and this desk does not describe that as a reason to upgrade. Nor does a 30%-growth company with a real, quantified tailwind and a clean trend warrant a downgrade on a forecast that has so far been revised in its favour. The rating moves when cost per device, cash flow or a contract-price call moves it.
