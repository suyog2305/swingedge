03 —

The Margin Transformation

Operating marginValue
Q1 FY26 (a year ago)30.33%
Q4 FY26 (last quarter)45.53%
Q1 FY2748.40%

A 48.4% operating margin is the highest of any company in this entire library — above Sudeep Pharma at 34.7%, Sanghvi Movers at 33.1% and Happy Forgings at 31.3%.

The reason is structural rather than cyclical, and it is the core of the investment case: solar generation has almost no marginal cost. Once a plant is built and connected, sunlight is free — there is no lignite to mine, no gas to buy, no fuel-price risk. Every rupee of solar revenue arrives at close to a 90% EBITDA margin.

So the margin move from 30.3% to 48.4% is essentially a mix shift from fuel-burning generation to solar. That is durable in a way a commodity-price margin is not, and it does not mean-revert. It is the single most important distinction between GIPCL and the chemicals names in this batch, whose margins are cyclical.

The offsetting reality: solar revenue is capped by installed capacity and irradiation. Growth requires building more plants, which is why Section 05 matters.

Two things make this more concrete than most capacity stories in your library.

First, the 600 MW is already fully commercialised — built, connected and generating. It is not a plan. The ₹420 crore of expected FY27 revenue addition is simply the arithmetic of a full year of operation replacing a partial one, which is mechanical.

Second, a ₹600 crore solar EBITDA target for FY27 against a ₹3,163 crore market capitalisation is a striking ratio. If delivered, the solar business alone would generate EBITDA equal to roughly 19% of the entire market value in one year.

The 500 MW under construction, commissioning post-monsoon in Q3 FY27, extends the same mechanism into FY28.

05 —

The ₹7,000 Crore Question

ProjectNew 750 MW lignite-based thermal plant
Indicated capital cost₹7,000 Cr
Relative to market capitalisation~2.2× the entire company

This is the largest single risk on this name and it deserves to be stated plainly. A company valued at ₹3,163 crore has outlined a thermal expansion costing more than twice that.

Three specific concerns follow:

  • Funding. ₹7,000 crore cannot come from a balance sheet this size without substantial debt, equity issuance, or both. Either dilutes the current shareholder or levers the company materially. The funding structure is the thing to establish before anything else.
  • Direction of travel. The margin transformation in Section 03 came from shifting toward solar. A ₹7,000 crore lignite plant shifts the mix decisively back toward fuel-burning generation — lower margin, fuel-price exposed, and carrying long-term carbon and regulatory risk.
  • Timeline. Thermal plants take years to build. Capital is committed and depreciation begins well before revenue does.

The tension is direct: the market is currently paying 6.3× earnings for a business whose margin doubled because it went solar, and management has proposed spending 2.2× the market capitalisation going back to lignite. That is a legitimate strategic choice for a state utility with baseload obligations — Gujarat needs dispatchable power — but it is not obviously value-accretive to a minority shareholder.

The financing structure, timeline, expected returns and board approval status of the ₹7,000 Cr project are not established in the sources reviewed. This is the highest-value item to obtain from the investor presentation.

The 0.83× book multiple is, in substantial part, the market pricing that second column. State-owned utilities across India trade at discounts to book for exactly these reasons, and GIPCL is not an exception to that pattern — it is an example of it.

09 —

Below Book

At 0.83× book, GIPCL trades below the accounting value of its own assets — the only name in this batch to do so, and one of only two in the entire library alongside Kiri Industries at 0.45×.

For an asset-heavy utility this is meaningful in a way it would not be for an asset-light business. GIPCL owns power plants, land and grid connections. The market is valuing the entire generating fleet — including 600 MW of freshly commissioned, near-zero-marginal-cost solar — at 83 paise on the rupee of stated book value.

The honest counterweight is that book value for a utility reflects historical cost of plant, some of which is ageing lignite and gas capacity whose economic life and future utilisation are genuinely uncertain in a decarbonising grid. Book value is not liquidation value here. But at a 48.4% operating margin and 175% profit growth, the discount is hard to justify purely on asset quality.

This is the most striking valuation in the batch and it is not close. A business earning a 48.4% operating margin, growing profit 175%, with capacity already built and generating, trading at 6.3× earnings and below book.

The market is telling you why, and the reasons are legible: it is a state undertaking with regulated tariffs, capped upside, minority shareholders with no say, and a proposed ₹7,000 crore thermal project worth 2.2× the market capitalisation. Utilities of this type do not re-rate on earnings alone; they re-rate when capital allocation becomes shareholder-friendly, or they do not re-rate at all.

The question is not whether GIPCL is cheap — it plainly is — but whether anything will close the gap. The 600 MW solar delivering ₹600 crore of EBITDA is the strongest candidate.

Scenarios

Bull
The 600 MW delivers the ₹600 Cr FY27 solar EBITDA target and the 500 MW commissions on schedule in Q3 FY27, carrying earnings into FY28. The 48.4% margin holds because it is structural, not cyclical. The ₹7,000 Cr lignite project is deferred, scaled down, or funded without dilution. A utility earning these returns re-rates from 0.83× book toward parity, closing much of the 25% gap to its all-time high.
Base
Solar delivers close to target and earnings grow strongly through FY27–28 on already-built capacity. But the state-undertaking discount persists, as it does across the sector, and the multiple stays low. Returns come from earnings growth rather than re-rating, which at 6.3× is still a respectable outcome.
Bear
The ₹7,000 Cr lignite plant proceeds and is funded through equity dilution or heavy leverage on a ₹3,163 Cr company. The mix shifts back toward fuel-burning generation, reversing the margin gain that drove the re-rating. Regulated tariffs cap any offset. The discount to book widens rather than closes, and the stock returns toward the ₹162–168 base it broke out of.

Key Risks

2.2× the market capitalisation, funding structure undisclosed. It would reverse the solar mix shift that produced the 1,807 bps margin gain, and cannot be funded from this balance sheet without dilution or substantial leverage.

Power tariffs are set by regulators under long-tenor PPAs. Unlike the chemicals names here, GIPCL cannot capture a price upcycle.

Solar revenue is capped by installed capacity. Beyond the 500 MW under construction, more growth means more capex — and the capex on the table is thermal.

06

07

State-owned utilities trade below book across India. Being cheap has not historically been sufficient to re-rate this cohort.
13 —

Summary

RATING: ACCUMULATE ON DIPS — CHEAPEST NAME IN THE BATCH

GIPCL delivered Q1 revenue +35%, EBITDA +112%, net profit +175%, and an operating margin of 48.4% — the highest of any company in this library. It is the third consecutive quarter of transformed profitability, and the cause is structural rather than cyclical: 600 MW of solar at Khavda, fully commercialised, generating at near-zero marginal cost.

It trades at 6.3× earnings and 0.83× book — below the accounting value of its own assets, and less than half the multiple of anything else on the breakout scan. A ₹600 crore FY27 solar EBITDA target against a ₹3,163 crore market capitalisation is a remarkable ratio, and the near-term pipeline is unusually de-risked: the capacity driving FY27 and FY28 is either built or under construction.

The discount has a legible cause, and it is not asset quality. This is a state undertaking with regulated tariffs, capped upside, and a proposed ₹7,000 crore lignite plant worth 2.2× the entire market capitalisation — a project that would reverse the very mix shift that doubled the margin. Whether that project proceeds, and how it is funded, matters more to the outcome here than anything in the quarterly numbers.
