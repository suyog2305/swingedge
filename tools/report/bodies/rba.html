02 —

The Model: Why QSR Franchises Take So Long

A quick-service restaurant franchisee is a fixed-cost business wearing a retail costume. Every store carries rent, staff, utilities and depreciation whether it serves 200 covers a day or 400. Above a threshold of average daily sales, incremental revenue drops almost straight to the operating line; below it, the store loses money.

That structure produces a characteristic shape: years of losses during expansion, then a sharp inflection once the store base matures and average daily sales cross the threshold. RBA has spent years in the first phase. The question this report addresses is whether Q1 FY27 marks the beginning of the second.

Two structural facts constrain the upside. First, as a franchisee rather than a brand owner, RBA pays royalties to Burger King and does not own the intellectual property it trades on. Second, its master franchise agreement carries store-opening obligations — it must keep building, which keeps consuming capital, whether or not conditions are ideal.

04 —

The SSSG Number — And Why Its Composition Matters More Than Its Size

Same-store sales growth of 12.6% in India, the best in fifteen quarters, is the headline. The more important disclosure is that it came entirely from traffic — more customers, not higher prices.

This distinction is the difference between a good quarter and a genuine turn:

  • Price-led SSSG is borrowed growth. Raising menu prices lifts the comparison for four quarters, then laps. It can also suppress footfall, storing up a problem.
  • Traffic-led SSSG is earned growth. More people choosing the brand indicates the offer is working — menu, value perception, execution, delivery. It compounds rather than laps, and it is the single most reliable indicator in restaurant retail.

A 12.6% traffic-led comp is the strongest operating fact in this report, and it is more meaningful than the revenue growth rate, because revenue can be bought with new store openings while comps cannot.

The caveat worth holding: fifteen quarters is nearly four years. That is how long this business has been waiting for a number like this, which tells you both that the turn is real and that it took a very long time to arrive.

Read the term “restaurant-level” carefully — it is doing significant work. Restaurant-level EBITDA measures profitability at the store, before corporate overhead, before head-office costs, before depreciation on the store build, and before interest and lease charges.

The arithmetic tells the story plainly: ₹90 crore of restaurant-level EBITDA in a quarter that produced a ₹28.35 crore net loss. Roughly ₹118 crore of costs sit between the store and the shareholder — corporate overhead, depreciation, and finance costs, of which lease accounting is typically the largest single component for a QSR chain.

That is the whole investment question in one line. The stores work. The company does not yet, and closing a ₹118 crore quarterly gap requires either substantially more restaurant-level profit or substantially less of everything above it.

06 —

Indonesia — The Drag Nobody Discusses

India grew 23.6%. Consolidated grew 18.0%. Of 752 total restaurants, 590 are Burger King India, leaving roughly 162 in Indonesia.

The arithmetic implies Indonesia grew far more slowly than India, and possibly not at all. Consolidated revenue of ₹823 crore less India’s ₹682 crore leaves approximately ₹141 crore from Indonesia — a business roughly a fifth the size of India that is expanding at a materially lower rate.

This is an inference from the two disclosed revenue figures rather than a reported Indonesia segment number. Treat the direction as reliable and the precise magnitude as approximate.

Indonesia matters for a reason beyond growth arithmetic: it is a second country with its own consumer cycle, currency, regulatory regime and competitive set, run by a management team whose primary attention is on the Indian turnaround. For a company that has not yet reached group profitability, a second geography absorbing capital and management bandwidth is a real cost, not a diversification benefit.

07 —

The Loss — Sizing the Distance to Break-Even

The quarterly net loss narrowed to ₹28.35 crore and EBITDA rose 266%. Both directions are right. Neither yet reaches zero.

A simple way to frame the remaining distance: with restaurant-level EBITDA margin at 13.2% on ₹823 crore of quarterly revenue, closing a ₹28.35 crore loss purely through revenue growth — holding the cost structure and margin constant — would require roughly ₹215 crore of additional quarterly revenue, about 26% growth from here.

This is a simplified illustration, not a forecast. It assumes incremental revenue converts at the current restaurant-level margin and that corporate costs, depreciation and finance charges stay flat — none of which will hold exactly. It is intended to show the order of magnitude of the gap.

At 23.6% India revenue growth, that distance is plausibly covered within roughly a year if the growth rate holds and costs do not rise with it. Both conditions are doing a lot of work in that sentence. The honest summary: break-even is visible from here for the first time in years, but it is not yet in hand.

Store count creates a tension specific to franchise agreements. New stores add revenue, but each one takes time to reach mature average daily sales — so a fast-expanding chain permanently carries a cohort of immature, loss-making stores that dilute the reported average.

The 12.6% same-store number is therefore doubly important: it strips out new-store effects and measures whether the existing base is improving. It is. But every new opening resets part of the drag, and master franchise agreements typically impose minimum opening commitments that limit management’s ability to pause building in order to reach profitability sooner.

With no earnings, the usual multiples do not apply, and that is the central valuation problem. Price-to-sales at 2.2× is undemanding for a branded consumer business. Price-to-book at 8.15× is high for one that loses money. The two measures point in opposite directions, which is typical of a pre-profitability turnaround and is precisely why these situations are hard to value.

The practical consequence for a holder: there is no earnings anchor, so the price is driven by the market’s changing expectation of when profitability arrives rather than by a multiple on anything real. That makes the shares more volatile than the operating business, in both directions — the near-20% move on a single result being the demonstration.

Average daily sales rise past the fixed-cost threshold, restaurant-level EBITDA scales, and the ₹118 Cr quarterly gap between store profit and net loss closes. Indian consumption slows, or QSR competition intensifies, and the 12.6% comp proves to be a peak rather than a base. Store-opening obligations keep consuming capital while comps soften — the worst combination for this model. With no earnings floor, 8.15× book and an ST-ASM flag, a stock that rose 76% off its low and 20% on one result can retrace quickly.

Summary

RATING: HOLD — A GENUINE INFLECTION, STILL BELOW THE LINE

Restaurant Brands Asia produced its best operating quarter in nearly four years: India revenue +23.6%, EBITDA +266%, and same-store sales growth of 12.6% that came entirely from traffic rather than from price. That last detail is what separates a real turn from a cosmetic one, and it is the most credible fact in this report.

The company nonetheless still loses money. ₹90 crore of restaurant-level EBITDA became a ₹28.35 crore net loss, with roughly ₹118 crore of overhead, depreciation and finance cost in between. It is the only holding you own without earnings, and therefore the only one with no valuation floor.

It is also the only one 54% below its all-time high — which cuts both ways. There is far more recovery room than in any other name you hold, and far more overhead supply to work through. Unlike the momentum names in your book, this is a recovery position: the thesis is measured in quarters of narrowing losses, and the specific event that would settle it is the first profitable quarter.
