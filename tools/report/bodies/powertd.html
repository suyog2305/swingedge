What India Has Announced, and the Other Tailwinds

The recently announced policy: PM-DHARA, approved 30 September 2026

The Union Cabinet approved Green Energy Corridor Phase III, branded PM-DHARA (PM-Developing Harmonized and Accelerated Renewable-energy Access), on 30 September 2026. It is a state-grid scheme: it strengthens the intra-state transmission system so that up to 135 GW of renewable energy can be evacuated, and it pays for 50 GWh of battery storage [6][7][8][9].

What it means for equipment makers is an inference, not a disclosed number. Because the money flows through state transmission utilities, orders will be placed by those utilities in competitive tenders, mostly for the 132, 220 and 400 kV classes they build. That points to 220 kV and 132/33 kV transformers, conductors, towers and substation EPC. It points less to the 765 kV and HVDC equipment bought by the central grid operator. The sources do not state the voltage mix. One fact from the company data fits the inference: at GE Vernova T&D India, state utilities are only 2% of the ₹20,930 crore backlog at 30 June 2026, against 77% private clients and 21% central utilities and public-sector buyers [24].

The longer-running plan it sits on

The CEA’s National Electricity Plan for transmission (published October 2024) sets a total outlay of ₹9.15 lakh crore to meet an expected peak demand of 458 GW by 2032: lines from 4.85 lakh to 6.48 lakh circuit-km, transformation capacity from 1,251 to 2,342 GVA, and nine new HVDC links of 33.25 GW [10][11]. A Draft National Electricity Policy 2026 was reported in January 2026, aiming at 2,000 kWh of electricity per head by 2030 [15]. Nothing read confirms the draft as final.

Was There an Electricity Shortage in September 2026?

Short answer: yes, on every press account found, and it was the largest monthly shortfall in about a decade. But the cause was fuel and water, not wires, and the primary monthly record for September was not yet published when this note was written.

Why it happened, as reported. Three causes appear together. Demand ran about 11% above the previous September, according to a Whalesbook headline [5]. Coal supply did not keep up: a CRISIL study cited by The Week found coal-rake loading up only 5% between April and August despite the rise in consumption, and prolonged rains disrupted eastern coal-belt mining and transport [2]. And a weak monsoon cut hydro output [3]. Coal still generates more than 70% of India’s electricity [2]. No source read attributes the September shortfall to a failure of transmission lines or transformers.

Why this matters for the stocks, and why it should not be over-read. The shortage raises the political priority of the whole power sector and sits two days either side of the PM-DHARA decision, but it is a fuel-supply event. A transformer maker’s order book is driven by utility tenders placed one to three years ahead; one month of coal shortfall does not change it. The Ember finding in §02, that renewable energy was being wasted for want of transmission, is the better evidence that wires are the constraint.

What could not be verified: the September all-India energy requirement and shortfall from CEA or Grid-India; a common-date count of critical-stock plants; and the percentage of requirement that 560 MU represents. The CEA September report is normally published after month-end and was not available.

Derived arithmetic, with a caveat. The 2032 plan needs transformation capacity to reach 2,342 GVA [10]. From 1,486 GVA in July 2026 that is another 856 GVA, or about 150 GVA a year over the 5.7 years to March 2032. FY26’s 113 GVA is three-quarters of that pace. The caveat: the 1,251 GVA and 1,486 GVA figures come from different sources and may not use the same voltage threshold, so this is an order-of-magnitude comparison. It says the plan needs a faster pace than the one delivered, which is the case for new transformer capacity.

A finding that cuts across the value chain. Transformation capacity was delivered at 90% of target while lines reached 47.5% in the first nine months. Q1 FY27 revenue grew between 8% (TARIL) and 69% (Hitachi Energy India) at the eight transformer and reactor makers with disclosed books (Shilchar, whose revenue fell, is the exception), while the four EPC and tower names grew 0% to 5% (§09) despite order books of 21 to 31 months of sales. On this evidence equipment is not the slow link; lines, sites and execution are.

Timing check, as the house rule requires. PM-DHARA was approved on 30 September 2026. Every quarterly figure in this note is for April–June 2026 (Q1 FY27) or, for Siemens Energy India, the quarter to June 2026 (its fiscal Q3 FY26). No reported number contains the policy. Order books reflect earlier plans — the 2024 transmission plan, central-grid renewable evacuation and export demand. Even the Q2 FY27 results due in October–November will precede any PM-DHARA tender.

The Outlook Business piece names raw-material availability and pricing as important, specifically that CRGO steel and copper are import-dependent, and skilled labour for winding, assembly and testing of large power transformers [12]. It draws on CEA, the Ministry of Power, Power Line magazine and Cushman & Wakefield and is a syndicated newswire page, so it is secondary.

Scenarios for the Theme

Three paths for the theme, not for any one stock, with the dated evidence that would move the reading. No probabilities are invented.

A — Plan delivered
State utilities issue PM-DHARA tenders within the next two to four quarters; FY27 additions reach the CEA targets; large-cap plants at Kalwa (mid-2027), Karjan (FY28) and Sehore ramp on schedule while order inflow stays above revenue. What would show it: published tenders, CEA monthly additions at or above target, and Q2 FY27 order inflow above revenue at Atlanta, Quality Power, TARIL and the large caps.
B — Plan slips on the ground
The FY26 pattern repeats: targets are cut, lines and sites lag factories, EPC revenue stays near flat while equipment makers keep reporting large books. What would show it: another downward revision of CEA annual targets, line additions again near half of target, and EPC order books (KEC, Kalpataru, Skipper, Transrail) growing while their revenue does not.
C — Capacity catches up with demand
Kalwa, Karjan, Sehore, Vadodara and several mid-cap plants arrive in 2027–2028 into state tenders open to many bidders, and 220 kV margins soften first. What would show it: falling order inflow per quarter, margin compression at the 220 kV-weighted makers, and utilisation disclosures at CG Power falling short of its capacity. GE Vernova T&D India’s 30% fall in Q1 order booking and 400 basis-point margin fall are the nearest early signals in the data [24].

Key Risks

01
Mistaking the September shortage for a transmission story
Every account found attributes it to coal logistics, weak hydro and demand; none to transformers or lines. Reading it as an order-book driver for the equipment makers would be an error, and the September figure itself rests on one press report until CEA publishes.
02
The delivery record is below the plan
FY26 transformation additions were 90% of a target that had itself been cut 36%; line additions to December were 47.5% of target [12][13][14].
03
State-utility tenders are competitive and may reach few of the large caps
PM-DHARA money flows through state utilities. Pricing power is lower at 132 and 220 kV, where many makers bid, than at 765 kV and HVDC; GE Vernova T&D India has 2% state-utility exposure in its backlog [24].
04
New capacity arrives together in 2027–2032
Kalwa, Karjan, Sehore and the mid-cap plants overlap. If they land at once, the premium for early capacity shrinks. CG Power’s Sehore plan is stated as 110,000 or 120,000 MVA in total in two sources.
05
Quality Power: execution at scale and a concentrated book
Sangli depends on statutory clearances and customer audits; 41% of the order book is in Turkey; management guides growth of about 20% against 32% in Q1 [22].
06
KSH International: copper-driven revenue and an unfinalised anchor customer
Revenue doubled on 30% volume growth; Q1 profit per tonne was above management’s own sustainable level; the Hitachi Energy framework has no fixed quantity or price yet [16].
07
Atlanta: qualification risk on the new plants and lumpy quarters
Two new plants at 39% and 15% utilisation depend on 400 kV and 765 kV prototypes passing; Q1 revenue was 38% below Q4 [18][44].
08
CRGO steel supply and trade remedies
About 90% of CRGO is imported and a 2026 anti-dumping probe could raise costs for every transformer maker [41][42].
09
Thin and secondary sourcing; surveillance unseen
Many company figures come from result summaries on trade and news sites; balance sheets were not retrieved for most names. Exchange surveillance status sits in the generated blocks and should be read there before assuming margin eligibility.

Summary

VERDICT: EVIDENCE RANKING AGAINST SEVEN STATED CRITERIA — NOT A RECOMMENDATION

The policy is real and recent; the shortage was real but is not a transmission story. PM-DHARA, approved on 30 September 2026, funds ₹1,86,405 crore of state-grid transmission and 50 GWh of storage by 2032–33 [6][8]. September 2026 saw about 560 million units not supplied on press accounts, the worst in about a decade, against 192 MU in July on CEA’s own data [1][3]; the reported causes are coal supply, a weak monsoon that cut hydro, and demand about 11% above last September [2][3][5]. The better transmission evidence is that India lost about 300 GWh of renewable energy to transmission limits in Q1 2026 [6].

The value chain’s tightest input is CRGO steel, and its strongest pricing power is at 765 kV and HVDC. Equipment is not the slow link: transformation capacity was delivered at 90% of target against 47.5% for lines, and the EPC names show flat revenue on order books of two years or more. Order books run 20 to 39 months at seven of the ten equipment makers with a disclosed book, and 9 to 13 months at Apar (conductors), Shilchar and Voltamp; KSH International’s is not disclosed.

The owner’s three. Atlanta Electricals ranks first of ten on the stated criteria, but its capacity is ahead of qualification, not of orders: new plants at 39% and 15% utilisation await 400 kV and 765 kV prototypes. Quality Power ranks sixth equal; its order book is strong, its Sangli plant is not yet confirmed running, and its valuation is high against growth. KSH International ranks eighth, mostly because its order book, grid share and balance sheet could not be tested.

The large-cap answer. Of GVT&D, Hitachi Energy India, CG Power, Siemens Energy India and Siemens, only CG Power has a large block of new transformer capacity already running (Mandideep at 65,000 MVA; Sehore’s first 10,000 MVA from 4 September 2026), and its utilisation is undisclosed. For the others, demand is ahead of capacity. The three highest-ranked names are Atlanta Electricals, Hitachi Energy India and GE Vernova T&D India; third place is not stable and the ranking rests on balance-sheet data that were missing for most names. This note carries no position-sizing, allocation or entry or exit view.
