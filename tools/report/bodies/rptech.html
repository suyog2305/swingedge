02 —

The Business: Distribution Is a Volume Machine

Rashi buys technology products from global brands and sells them onward to India’s reseller channel. It is logistics, credit and working capital wearing a technology label.

The economics are unambiguous from the numbers: ₹5,102 crore of quarterly revenue producing a 3.04% operating margin. For every ₹100 of goods that pass through, roughly ₹3 stays behind before interest, depreciation and tax. That is the structural reality of distribution, and it defines everything about how the company should be valued.

What a distributor actually sells

  • Reach. A global brand entering India cannot economically build its own channel to thousands of resellers. Rashi already has it.
  • Credit. Resellers buy on terms. The distributor finances that, which is why working capital dominates the balance sheet.
  • Logistics and availability. Holding stock so the channel does not have to.

None of these confer pricing power. Brands set prices, resellers negotiate hard, and the margin is the thin spread in between. A distributor grows by moving more boxes, and improves by moving better boxes — which is exactly the strategy management has articulated.

04 —

Margin Compressed While Revenue Grew 62%

Revenue rose 61.9%. EBITDA rose 50.0%. That gap means the operating margin fell — from roughly 3.28% to 3.04%, a compression of about 24 basis points.

This is the single most important observation in the report, and it runs directly counter to the headline. In almost every other company in this batch — Happy Forgings, IOL Chemicals, Divgi, KRN, Ramkrishna — revenue growth came with margin expansion. Here it came with margin contraction.

Three explanations are plausible, and they have very different implications:

  • Mix. Growth came disproportionately from lower-margin, high-volume product lines. Benign, and consistent with a large PC or component cycle.
  • Price competition. Rashi bought share by discounting. Less benign, and a warning about the channel.
  • Cost of scaling. Adding logistics and credit capacity ahead of the revenue. Temporary if true.

At a 3% margin, 24 basis points is not a rounding error — it is roughly 8% of the entire operating margin. In thin-margin distribution, small spread movements have outsized effects on profit, and this is the number that determines whether 62% revenue growth is worth having.

05 —

Why PAT Grew Faster Than EBITDA

There is a second, subtler anomaly. EBITDA rose 50.0% while PAT rose 69.5%. Profit after tax grew faster than operating profit, which means the improvement came from below the operating line.

Mechanically there are only a few candidates: lower interest cost, higher other income, or a lower effective tax rate.

For a distributor, interest is the most likely explanation and the most encouraging one. Distribution is financed with working-capital debt; if Rashi has reduced borrowing or refinanced more cheaply, a falling interest charge would lift PAT faster than EBITDA, and that benefit is durable.

If instead the gain came from other income or a tax adjustment, it is one-time and the underlying earnings growth is closer to the 50% EBITDA figure than the 69.5% headline.

This report does not have the interest and tax lines from the results to determine which applies. It is flagged because the distinction changes the quality of the earnings materially, and it is directly checkable in the quarterly filing.

06 —

The Enterprise and AI Shift — The Real Thesis

Management has been explicit about the strategy: moving from commodity components toward enterprise technology, digital infrastructure and advanced solutions. The company added WEKA to its Enterprise vertical, expanding into AI, machine-learning and high-performance-computing workloads.

Understand why this matters more than the revenue growth. Distributing a graphics card is a 2% margin business. Distributing and supporting enterprise AI infrastructure is a 6–10% margin business, because it requires pre-sales engineering, solution design and post-sales support that the reseller channel cannot provide alone.

If Rashi can shift even a modest share of its ₹5,102 crore quarterly revenue into higher-margin enterprise lines, the effect on profit is disproportionate. Moving the blended operating margin from 3.04% to 4.00% — still thin in absolute terms — would raise quarterly EBITDA by roughly a third at constant revenue.

That is the thesis: not revenue growth, which is already strong, but margin mix. And the Q1 margin compression is precisely the evidence that it has not yet happened.

08 —

Working Capital — The Real Balance Sheet

For a distributor, the balance sheet is the business. Revenue of ₹5,102 crore in a quarter means roughly ₹20,000 crore of annual goods flow, financed through inventory and receivables against a market capitalisation of ₹5,422 crore.

Two consequences follow, and both are heightened by 62% revenue growth:

  • Growth consumes cash. Every incremental rupee of sales requires more inventory and more receivables. A distributor growing 62% is absorbing working capital at a rate that can outpace its profit.
  • Receivable quality is the hidden risk. Rashi extends credit to resellers. In a downturn, channel defaults hit a business earning a 3% margin far harder than one earning 30%.

The single most useful check on this company is operating cash flow against reported PAT. If ₹104.6 crore of quarterly profit is accompanied by materially negative operating cash flow, the growth is being funded rather than generated — the classic distribution trap, and the reason low P/E ratios in this sector are sometimes justified rather than cheap.

On every conventional measure Rashi is dramatically the cheapest company you own. 17.0× trailing earnings against a book where the median is above 60×. 2.67× book against 21.8× at the top. A quarter of a year’s revenue as market capitalisation.

The discipline is to understand why, because a low multiple in distribution is often correct rather than mispriced. A business earning 3.04% on revenue, carrying large working capital, exposed to channel credit risk and holding no pricing power deserves a lower multiple than a 31%-margin manufacturer with a qualification moat. Price-to-sales of 0.27× looks striking until you remember that 97% of those sales are cost.

The genuine question is whether 17.0× is too cheap even for what this is — a company growing revenue 62%, profit 69.5%, and actively executing a mix shift toward higher-margin enterprise work. That case is reasonable. What would confirm it is the margin turning back up, and Q1 showed the opposite.

Summary

RATING: ACCUMULATE ON DIPS — CHEAPEST NAME YOU OWN

Rashi Peripherals is the most interesting valuation in your portfolio and the one that least resembles everything else you hold. Where KRN trades at 106.7× earnings and TD Power at 21.8× book, Rashi trades at 17.0× earnings and 2.67× book while growing revenue 62% and profit 69.5%. On annualised Q1 earnings it is closer to 13×.

Two findings temper that, and both come from reading the quarter rather than the headline. Margin compressed while revenue grew — EBITDA up 50% against revenue up 62% — which is the opposite of every other company in this batch and undercuts the enterprise-mix thesis it is supposed to support. And PAT grew faster than EBITDA, meaning part of the profit growth came from below the operating line and may not be repeatable.

The market has noticed something: this is the only holding you own that is 15.6% below its high, and it fell 10.8% in a week on a record result. That combination — genuinely cheap, genuinely growing, and being sold — is either the best opportunity in your book or a warning the numbers have not yet surfaced. The cash flow statement settles it, and it is the single most valuable thing you could read on this name.
