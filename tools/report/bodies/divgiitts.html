02 —

The Business

A transfer case is the gearbox that splits torque between the front and rear axles in a four-wheel-drive vehicle. It is a safety-relevant, precision-engineered assembly, and it is the kind of component where design validation runs for years and a supplier, once designed in, tends to stay for the life of the vehicle programme.

The moat is real but narrow

Divgi is one of a small number of Indian companies with the capability to design and manufacture transfer cases. That is a genuine technical barrier — far more so than, say, making a coil or a forging. Programme wins are sticky and multi-year.

The narrowness is the problem. A transfer case is only needed on 4WD and AWD vehicles, which are a minority of the Indian market. The addressable pool is therefore structurally limited, and growth requires either winning a larger share of a small market, expanding into adjacent driveline components, or exporting. Divgi is attempting all three.

The Margin Jump — 450 Basis Points in One Year

EBITDA margin moved from 24.9% to 29.4%, and PAT margin from 11.6% to 17.8%. A 620 basis point expansion in net margin in twelve months is unusually large, and understanding its source determines whether it is repeatable.

Management points to mix and operating leverage. Both are credible for this business:

  • Operating leverage. At ₹77 crore of quarterly income the fixed-cost base was poorly covered. At ₹142 crore it is well covered. In a precision-manufacturing business with heavy tooling and validation costs, that alone explains a large share of the margin move.
  • Mix. Higher-value transfer cases and export components carry better realisations than base component supply.

The caution that applies to every operating-leverage story: the same mechanism that produced +450 basis points on the way up produces a comparable move on the way down. At this revenue scale, a single programme pausing takes a disproportionate share of volume with it — and margin follows volume immediately.

A 29.4% EBITDA margin also puts Divgi close to Happy Forgings’ 31.3%, which is a demanding standard for a company one-sixth its size. That is a genuine achievement and a reason for caution about extrapolating it.

05 —

The Five-Year Strategic Order

Divgi secured a major five-year strategic order in June 2026, which management describes as the principal source of forward revenue visibility.

This is the most important development in the story and also the one with the least public detail. In the sources reviewed, the order’s value and customer have not been quantified in a way that can be reported here. This report will not estimate it.

What can be said about its structure:

  • A five-year automotive supply agreement is genuinely valuable — it typically means being designed into a vehicle programme, which is difficult to displace.
  • For a company with ₹142 crore of quarterly revenue, a single strategic order large enough to be described as the main visibility driver implies meaningful concentration in one customer and one programme.
  • Programme risk is real: vehicle programmes get delayed, re-specified or cancelled, and a supplier carries that risk without controlling it.

The order is a strength and a concentration simultaneously. Getting the actual value and counterparty from the company’s disclosures is the highest-value piece of homework on this name.

“Under development and launch” and “quoting stage” are not orders. Pipeline figures in auto components convert at well below 100% and over multi-year timeframes. Treat this as an indication of direction, not as contracted revenue.

The Scale Problem

At ₹3,554 crore of market capitalisation and ₹142 crore of quarterly revenue, Divgi is materially the smallest company in your holdings — roughly half the size of the next smallest, and a twenty-fourth of MCX.

Three practical consequences follow, and for a leveraged holder they compound:

  • Liquidity. Smaller companies trade thinner. Exiting a position of meaningful size takes longer and moves the price more, which is precisely the problem during a fast decline.
  • Single-programme sensitivity. One customer programme is a large share of a ₹142 crore quarter. Concentration risk that would be immaterial at HFCL’s scale is existential here.
  • Volatility. The stock is up 104% from its low and already 12% off its high — a wider trading range than the larger names in your book.
09 —

The EV Transition — Two-Sided

Divgi’s core product exists to distribute torque from an internal combustion engine through a mechanical driveline. Electrification changes that architecture, and the effect on Divgi is genuinely ambiguous rather than simply negative:

  • Against: Some EV architectures use dual motors — one per axle — and eliminate the mechanical transfer case entirely. Where that design wins, the core product disappears from the vehicle.
  • For: Other EV architectures retain a single motor with mechanical distribution, and EV drivelines need new precision components — reduction gearboxes, couplers, disconnect systems — that play to the same engineering capability. The company’s engagement with European EV customers indicates it is competing for exactly this.

The honest position is that this is unresolved and will be for years. It is a reason to watch the EV component order wins closely rather than a reason to assume decline — but it is a structural question that does not apply to most of the other names you hold.

Summary

RATING: HOLD — BEST QUARTER YET, SMALLEST BALANCE SHEET

Divgi delivered the highest-quality quarter of any name in this batch on a percentage basis: income +85%, EBITDA +118%, PAT +183%, EBITDA margin +450 basis points to 29.4%, and PAT margin +620 basis points. For a company operating in a genuinely defensible engineering niche, that is an excellent result.

It is also the smallest company you own by a wide margin, with ₹142 crore of quarterly revenue, visibility resting on a five-year order whose value has not been disclosed, a pipeline that is indicative rather than contracted, and an unresolved long-term question about whether EV architectures need its core product at all.

The most interesting fact is the divergence: the best profit growth in the batch, and a share price 11.9% below its high and falling on the week. That gap either represents an opportunity the market has not priced, or it reflects something in the detail — programme timing, order specifics, or simply thin liquidity — that the headline numbers do not capture. Resolving which requires the order value, and that is the one number this report could not source.
